    \section{Summary: Optical Switched Networks vs.\ Other Computer Networks}
    \label{sec:osn_summary}

    Optical Switched Networks (OSNs) --- the family that includes the three canonical designs studied here, Opera~\cite{opera}, Shale~\cite{shale}, Sirius~\cite{sirius}, and the GA-evolved topologies introduced in \S\ref{sec:ga} --- move packet forwarding out of the electrical domain and into optical circuits established between Top-of-Rack (ToR) nodes.  They differ from the dominant alternatives --- traditional electrical packet-switched fabrics (\emph{Clos}, \emph{fat-tree}, \emph{leaf--spine}), direct-connect networks (\emph{torus}, \emph{HyperX}, \emph{Dragonfly}), and hybrid optical/electrical designs --- on five axes that drive both the benchmarking framework (\S1.2) and the architectural choices throughout this paper.

    \paragraph{OSN pros (advantages over electrical / hybrid networks).}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Power and cost efficiency.}  Eliminating electrical packet switches at the network core cuts power by $2$--$4\times$ for equivalent bandwidth; Sirius reports $74$--$77\%$ lower power than a non-blocking electrical fabric of the same size~\cite{sirius}.
        \item \textbf{Line-rate independence.}  Optical circuits carry any modulation format transparently, so upgrading link speed (e.g.\ $100\,\text{Gb/s}\to 400\,\text{Gb/s}$) does not require replacing the core switching fabric.
        \item \textbf{Low and predictable latency for in-circuit traffic.}  When a flow traverses a direct optical circuit, there is no packet-level queuing inside the core --- propagation plus a fixed guardband dominate (${\sim}100\,\text{ns}$ for Sirius, ${\sim}200\,\text{ns}$ for Shale).  Electrical Clos fabrics add nondeterministic queuing at each hop.
        \item \textbf{High bisection bandwidth at scale.}  Reconfigurable optical fabrics deliver full bisection to arbitrary demand matrices within a cycle (Opera), while traditional fat-trees require $k$-port switches that scale poorly beyond a few thousand ToRs.
        \item \textbf{Topology flexibility via reconfiguration.}  The circuit-schedule or GA-evolved graph can be tailored to workload, whereas Clos topology is fixed at build time.
    \end{itemize}

    \paragraph{OSN cons (disadvantages relative to traditional networks).}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Reconfiguration gap.}  Every circuit-switched design pays a duty-cycle loss $\delta/T$ (\S1.2.5) that electrical packet switches do not incur; depending on the hardware generation this is $3.84\,\text{ns}$ (Sirius), up to $10\,\mu\text{s}$ (Opera), or $\approx 0$ for static photonic wiring.
        \item \textbf{Adversarial fragility.}  As \S\ref{sec:adversarial_skew} shows, each OSN has a \emph{routing-structural} critical load $\rho^*$ (Sirius $0.85$, Opera $0.883$, Shale $1/(h{+}1)$) above which throughput collapses.  Electrical Clos fabrics have graceful degradation without a sharp cliff.
        \item \textbf{Bandwidth tax from multi-hop routing.}  Short flows detoured through VLB intermediates (Sirius, Shale) or multi-hop expander paths (Opera) consume $1-1/\ell$ of extra link time without delivering useful data (\S1.2.4).  Packet-switched fat-trees deliver every byte on the ingress--egress shortest path.
        \item \textbf{Oblivious scheduling.}  Opera and Sirius use demand-\emph{oblivious} round-robin schedules~\cite{opera,sirius}, which means they cannot adapt to traffic imbalance within a cycle.  Modern electrical fabrics run demand-aware ECMP/adaptive routing and redistribute load continuously.
        \item \textbf{Buffering constraints.}  OSNs either eliminate buffers in the optical core (Sirius~\cite{sirius}) or rely on edge buffering (Opera, Shale), so bursts that exceed the configured buffer depth cause queue divergence at VLB intermediaries --- the failure mode Sirius-adversarial (\S\ref{sec:adv_sirius}) exploits.
    \end{itemize}

    \paragraph{How these guidelines shape the GA design.}
    The evolved GA-Robust topology (\S\ref{sec:ga}) is deliberately constructed to \emph{adhere to the OSN pros and avoid the cons}:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \emph{Static photonic wiring} $\Rightarrow$ \textbf{hw\_reconfig\_ratio $=0$}: the reconfiguration-gap con vanishes (same structural advantage as Shale; better than Opera's $4\%$ and Sirius's $3.84\%$).
        \item \emph{Multi-objective fitness on $\{\text{Uniform}, \text{Skewed}, \text{Hotspot}\}$}: evolves short paths that avoid the adversarial-fragility con (\S\ref{sec:adv_ga}, \S\ref{sec:arch_pure_summary}).
        \item \emph{Target degree $D$ tunable to ToR radix}: preserves the power and line-rate pros (the evolved graph is buildable with the same number of fibres per ToR as Opera or Sirius).
        \item \emph{No forced VLB spray}: any source--destination pair routes on its \emph{shortest} path on the evolved graph, sidestepping the bandwidth-tax con.
        \item \emph{No demand-oblivious schedule}: the GA is demand-\emph{informed} at evolve time (the fitness reads the traffic distribution), converting the oblivious-scheduling con into a design-time optimisation step.
    \end{itemize}
    The explicit construction of the GA fitness (\S\ref{sec:ga}) and the dynamic routing variant (\S\ref{sec:ga_dynamic}) both inherit these guidelines; any future architecture or modification to the code base should be evaluated against this five-axis pros/cons list before being accepted.

    \section{Benchmarking Methodology}
    We evaluate our network architectures using a unified benchmarking framework that implements a complete feedback loop: \textbf{Benchmark Scenario $\to$ Experiment Configuration $\to$ Metrics $\to$ Benchmark Score}.

    \subsection{Benchmark Scenario}
    The network is stressed under various traffic models and failure conditions:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Traffic Models} (implemented in \texttt{Traffic\_Benchmarks.py}): Demand matrix $D[i,j]$ for $i \neq j$; $D[i,i]=0$.
            \begin{itemize}
                \item \textbf{Uniform}: $D[i,j] = 1$ (all pairs equal).
                \item \textbf{Skewed}: Zipf/power-law over randomised node ranks.  Each source $i$ draws a uniformly random permutation $\pi_i$ of the other $N{-}1$ destinations; demand to the rank-$r$ destination is
                \[
                    D[i,j] = \frac{1}{r_{\pi_i}(j)^{\,\gamma} + 1}, \quad r_{\pi_i}(j) \in \{1,\ldots,N{-}1\}
                \]
                with $\gamma$ the Zipf exponent (default 2.0).  Using a random permutation per source means each seed produces a \emph{different spatial pattern} while preserving the same marginal Zipf distribution — addressing the limitation that a fixed index-distance formula always places the same pair of nodes in the same ``locality'' class.

                \textbf{Exact skew ratio} (max to min demand per source):
                \[
                    \frac{D_{\max}}{D_{\min}} = \frac{1/(1^\gamma+1)}{1/((N{-}1)^\gamma+1)} = \frac{(N{-}1)^\gamma+1}{2} \approx \frac{(N{-}1)^\gamma}{2}
                \]
                For $N=9$, $\gamma=2.0$: ratio $\approx 32\times$ ($D_{\max}\approx0.50$, $D_{\min}\approx0.015$).
                For $N=9$, $\gamma=1.0$: ratio $\approx 4\times$ ($D_{\max}\approx0.50$, $D_{\min}\approx0.111$).
                \item \textbf{Hotspot}: $D[i,j] = \omega$ if $i \in \mathcal{H}$ or $j \in \mathcal{H}$, else $1$; $\mathcal{H}$ hotspot nodes, $\omega$ intensity (e.g.\ 10).
                \item \textbf{Adversarial}: $D[i, j(i)] = N$ for $j(i) = (i + \lfloor N/2 \rfloor) \bmod N$, $i \neq j(i)$; all other entries $0$.
            \end{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Load Factor Sweep}: We sweep the load $L$ from light load (0.05) to saturation (0.95).
    \end{itemize}

    \subsection{Performance Metrics Definition}
    We rigorously define the performance metrics used to evaluate each architecture.

    \subsubsection{Normalized Throughput ($\mathcal{T}_{norm}$)}
    In the benchmarking framework (\texttt{Traffic\_Benchmarks.py}), all rates are expressed as a fraction of the per-link line rate $R$, and the implementation takes $R = 1$ without loss of generality. Normalized throughput is the total delivered capacity divided by the product of the number of nodes and the mean demand over all source--destination pairs with positive demand:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{T}_{norm} = \frac{\sum_{(i,j)} r_{\text{eff}}(i,j)}{N \cdot \bar{D}}
    \end{equation}
    where $N$ is the number of nodes, $\bar{D} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}|}\sum_{(i,j) \in \mathcal{F}} D_{i,j}$ is the mean demand (averaged over the set $\mathcal{F}$ of pairs with $D_{i,j} > 0$; $D$ is the demand matrix from \S1.1), and $r_{\text{eff}}(i,j)$ is the effective rate delivered for pair $(i,j)$ after the architecture-specific capacity model.

    \paragraph{Effective rate $r_{\text{eff}}$.}
    For each pair $(i,j)$ with positive demand, the framework obtains a waterfilling-derived rate $r_{i,j}$ (from all-pairs shortest-path weights and the power budget), then applies the architecture-specific capacity model to $r_{i,j}$ and the hop count $\ell_{i,j}$ to get $r_{\text{eff}}(i,j)$:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Shale~\cite{shale}:} $r_{\text{eff}} = r / u_{\max}$, where $u_{\max} = \max_{(a,b)\in E}\; \sum_{f \ni (a,b)} D_f / k_f$ is the maximum directed-link load when each flow~$f$ with demand~$D_f$ is split uniformly across~$k_f$ diverse spray paths found by penalised Dijkstra (\texttt{spray\_short}). This reflects Shale's Valiant Load Balancing (VLB) routing, where traffic is sprayed through $h$ intermediate nodes to ensure uniform load distribution across all links~\cite{shale}.
        \item \textbf{Opera~\cite{opera}:} $r_{\text{eff}} = \alpha \cdot r \cdot (1 - \delta/T_{\text{cycle}}) + (1-\alpha) \cdot r / \max(1,\ell)$ (bulk fraction $\alpha$, reconfig delay $\delta$, cycle $T_{\text{cycle}}$; short flows penalized by hop count $\ell$). Opera's key insight is that the vast majority of bytes reside in bulk flows~\cite{opera}; by buffering these until a direct 1-hop circuit is available, Opera eliminates the bandwidth tax on most traffic while routing the small fraction of latency-sensitive flows over a time-varying expander graph~\cite{opera}.
        \item \textbf{Sirius~\cite{sirius}:} $r_{\text{eff}} = \dfrac{\eta \cdot r}{\mathcal{H}(\ell)} \cdot C_{\text{load}}(\rho)$, where the hop denominator is
        \[
            \mathcal{H}(\ell) =
            \begin{cases}
                1       & \ell = 1 \text{ (direct scheduling)}\\
                2       & \ell = 2 \text{ (Valiant detour)}\\
                \ell^2  & \ell \geq 3 \text{ (multi-hop)}
            \end{cases}
        \]
        scheduling efficiency $\eta = (T_{slot} - \delta)/T_{slot} = (100\,\text{ns} - 3.84\,\text{ns})/100\,\text{ns} = 0.9616$~\cite{sirius}, and the load penalty is $C_{\text{load}}(\rho) = \exp\!\bigl(-10\,(\rho - 0.85)\bigr)$ when $\rho > 0.85$, else $1$. Sirius achieves nanosecond-granularity reconfiguration through custom tunable laser chips with sub-912\,ps tuning latency, enabling $3.84\,\text{ns}$ end-to-end reconfiguration atop $50\,\text{Gbps}$ channels~\cite{sirius}.
    \end{itemize}

    \subsubsection{Flow Completion Time ($FCT_{norm}$)}
    The normalized Flow Completion Time (FCT) measures the time to complete a unit flow relative to the ideal transmission time.
    \begin{equation}
        FCT_{norm} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}|} \sum_{f \in \mathcal{F}} \frac{t_{end}(f) - t_{start}(f)}{S_f / R}
    \end{equation}
    where $\mathcal{F}$ is the set of flows, $t$ represents timestamps, and $S_f$ is the flow size.

    \subsubsection{Mean Latency ($\bar{\tau}$)}
    Mean latency captures the average packet delay, including serialization, propagation, and queuing/reconfiguration delays.
    \begin{equation}
        \bar{\tau} = \frac{1}{N_{pkts}} \sum_{p} (T_{prop} + T_{queue} + T_{reconfig})
    \end{equation}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Shale:} $\tau \approx \gamma \cdot \bar{\ell}_{\text{spray}} + \beta_0$, where $\bar{\ell}_{\text{spray}}$ is the mean hop count across the spray paths actually used, $\gamma = 1.2$ ($6.76\,\text{ns}$ per hop at $T_{slot} = 5.632\,\text{ns}$) is the per-hop cycle overhead, and $\beta_0 = 2.5$ ($14.08\,\text{ns}$) is the base processing delay. For example, with $h=4$: $\tau \approx 1.2 \times 4 + 2.5 = 7.3$ normalized units $\approx 41.1\,\text{ns}$. Amir et al.\ show that Shale achieves a maximum intrinsic latency of $O(h\sqrt[h]{N})$ timeslots and worst-case throughput of $1/(2h)$ of line rate for $h$ spray hops, a Pareto optimal tradeoff for ORN designs~\cite{shale}.
        \item \textbf{Opera:} At low load, $\tau \approx \ell \cdot \varepsilon$ where $\varepsilon = 90\,\mu\text{s}$ is the topology-slice duration~\cite{opera}. At higher load, bulk traffic queues for its scheduled direct circuit, adding an M/D/1 queuing component:
        \[
            \tau(\ell, \rho) \approx \ell \cdot \delta + \min\!\left(\frac{\rho_{\text{bulk}} \cdot T_{\text{cycle}}}{2(1-\rho_{\text{bulk}})},\; 5\,T_{\text{cycle}}\right),
            \qquad \rho_{\text{bulk}} = \alpha \cdot \frac{\rho}{\alpha(1-\delta/T_c)}
        \]
        where $\rho_{\text{bulk}}$ is the offered load normalised to the bulk-circuit capacity ceiling $\alpha(1-\delta/T_c) \approx 0.883$.  At low load the queuing term is negligible; above the ceiling it grows steeply (M/D/1 divergence).  The effective bulk fraction also decreases above the ceiling as traffic overflows to the expander, raising bandwidth tax.  In our normalised model: $\delta = 2.0$, $T_{\text{cycle}} = 50.0$ (abstract units); physical: $\delta \approx 10\,\mu\text{s}$, $T_{\text{cycle}} \approx 250\,\mu\text{s}$.  Mellette et al.\ report that Opera delivers flow completion times comparable to cost-equivalent static topologies for latency-sensitive traffic, while achieving up to $4\times$ throughput for shuffle workloads~\cite{opera}.
        \item \textbf{Sirius:} $\tau \approx \ell / \eta$, where $\eta = 1 - \delta/T_{slot} = 1 - 3.84\,\text{ns}/100\,\text{ns} = 0.9616$ is the scheduling efficiency~\cite{sirius}. Each hop consumes one $100\,\text{ns}$ timeslot, but only $96.16\,\text{ns}$ carries data, so the effective per-hop cost is $1/\eta \approx 104\,\text{ns}$. Sirius is fundamentally a 2-hop system (source$\to$intermediate$\to$destination via Valiant Load Balancing), giving $\tau \approx 2/\eta \approx 208\,\text{ns}$. Ballani et al.\ show that by eliminating buffers in the optical core and carefully managing edge buffering, Sirius achieves very low and predictable latency~\cite{sirius}. Additional latency from propagation (up to $2.5\,\mu\text{s}$ for $500\,\text{m}$ detouring) and queuing at intermediate nodes (up to $1.6\,\mu\text{s}$ per epoch of $16 \times 100\,\text{ns}$) may also apply. Multi-hop ($\ell \geq 3$) paths do not appear in~\cite{sirius} but are modeled in our framework for generality.
    \end{itemize}

    \subsubsection{Bandwidth Tax ($\mathcal{B}_{tax}$)}
    Bandwidth tax quantifies the fraction of link capacity consumed by multi-hop forwarding that does not deliver useful data to the final destination.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Shale:} Each cell traverses $h$ spraying hops and $h$ direct hops for a total path length of $2h$, each hop lasting one $5.632\,\text{ns}$ timeslot~\cite{shale}. The worst-case throughput is $1/(2h)$ of line rate~\cite{shale}, giving:
        \[
            \mathcal{B}_{tax}^{\text{Shale}} = 1 - \frac{1}{2h}
        \]
        For $h=1$: $\mathcal{B}_{tax} = 0.5$ ($2 \times 5.632 = 11.26\,\text{ns}$ total path time). For $h=4$: $\mathcal{B}_{tax} = 0.875$ ($8 \times 5.632 = 45.06\,\text{ns}$).
        \item \textbf{Opera:} Only the $(1-\alpha)$ fraction of traffic routed over the expander pays a multi-hop tax~\cite{opera}. With average expander path length $L_{\text{avg}}$:
        \[
            \mathcal{B}_{tax}^{\text{Opera}} = (1-\alpha) \cdot \frac{L_{\text{avg}} - 1}{L_{\text{avg}}}
        \]
        Bulk traffic ($\alpha = 0.92$) takes direct 1-hop circuits and pays zero tax. Mellette et al.\ report an effective $8.4\%$ aggregate bandwidth tax rate for published skewed datacenter workloads~\cite{opera}.
        \item \textbf{Sirius:} All traffic is detoured through exactly one intermediate node (2-hop VLB)~\cite{sirius}, consuming twice the link bandwidth per logical transfer:
        \[
            \mathcal{B}_{tax}^{\text{Sirius}} = 1 - \frac{1}{2} = 0.5
        \]
        Each detour consumes $2 \times 100\,\text{ns} = 200\,\text{ns}$ of link time to deliver $100\,\text{ns}$ worth of data. Ballani et al.\ note that throughput ``can be at most $2\times$ worse than that of an ideal, non-blocking network''~\cite{sirius}. In our generalized model, $\ell \geq 3$ paths pay $\mathcal{B}_{tax} = 1 - 1/\ell$.
    \end{itemize}

    \subsubsection{Duty Cycle Loss ($\mathcal{D}_{loss}$)}
    Duty cycle loss represents the fraction of time the network is unavailable due to reconfiguration.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Shale:} $\mathcal{D}_{loss} \approx 0$. The oblivious schedule runs back-to-back timeslots ($5.632\,\text{ns}$ each) with no reconfiguration gap, enabled by nanosecond-scale clock synchronization and careful propagation delay control~\cite{shale}.
        \item \textbf{Opera:} Reconfigurations are staggered across $u$ rotor switches so that only one switch is down at a time~\cite{opera}. The raw reconfiguration delay $r$ is amortized over the inter-reconfiguration period $u \cdot \varepsilon$:
        \[
            \mathcal{D}_{loss}^{\text{Opera}} = \frac{r}{u \cdot \varepsilon}
        \]
        For the 108-rack example in~\cite{opera} ($r \approx 10\,\mu\text{s}$, $u = 6$, $\varepsilon = 90\,\mu\text{s}$): $\mathcal{D}_{loss} = 10/540 \approx 1.85\%$, yielding a ${\sim}98\%$ duty cycle. In our normalized model: $\delta / T_{\text{cycle}} = 2.0/50.0 = 4\%$.
        \item \textbf{Sirius:} Each timeslot loses $\delta = 3.84\,\text{ns}$ to laser retuning out of $T_{slot} = 100\,\text{ns}$~\cite{sirius}:
        \[
            \mathcal{D}_{loss}^{\text{Sirius}} = \frac{\delta}{T_{slot}} = 0.0384 = 1 - \eta
        \]
    \end{itemize}

    \subsubsection{Benchmark Score ($S_{bench}$)}
    The composite benchmark score combines the five metrics from \S1.2.1--1.2.5 into a single figure of merit. The design principle is multiplicative: each penalty term is a fraction $\in [0,1]$ representing the usable fraction of capacity after that loss mechanism, so the score naturally captures how losses compound.
    \begin{equation}
        S_{bench} = \mathcal{T}_{norm} \cdot (1 - \mathcal{B}_{tax}) \cdot (1 - \mathcal{D}_{loss}) \cdot \frac{1}{1 + \bar{\tau}} \cdot \frac{1}{1 + \ln(FCT_{norm})}
    \end{equation}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $\mathcal{T}_{norm}$: normalized throughput (\S1.2.1) --- how much demand is satisfied.
        \item $(1 - \mathcal{B}_{tax})$: useful fraction of consumed bandwidth (\S1.2.4) --- penalizes multi-hop overhead.
        \item $(1 - \mathcal{D}_{loss})$: fraction of time the network is active (\S1.2.5) --- penalizes reconfiguration downtime.
        \item $1/(1 + \bar{\tau})$: latency discount (\S1.2.3) --- maps $\bar{\tau} \in [0, \infty)$ to $(0, 1]$; zero latency gives full credit.
        \item $1/(1 + \ln FCT_{norm})$: completion-time efficiency (\S1.2.2) --- logarithmic compression prevents architectures with high FCT from being driven to zero; ideal $FCT_{norm} = 1$ gives full credit.
    \end{itemize}
    All five factors are dimensionless. The multiplicative structure means a zero in any factor (e.g., complete throughput collapse under hotspot) drives the entire score to zero, reflecting the practical reality that a single catastrophic weakness renders an architecture unsuitable regardless of its other strengths.
    \section{Shale}
    Shale~\cite{shale} is an Oblivious Reconfigurable Network (ORN) that generalizes earlier designs (RotorNet, Shoal, Sirius~\cite{sirius}) to achieve a tunable tradeoff between throughput and latency scaling. $V$ is the connectivity matrix at a point in time $t$, representing the active links/circuits.
    \[
        V = \mathscr{M}_{n \times n}
    \]
    We specifically define the entries of the adjacency/connectivity matrix $V$ such that $V_{ij} = 1$ if a link exists between $i$ and $j$, and $V_{ij} = 0$ implies no connection.
    
    $D$ is the vector of the total volume of traffic sending from each node, represented as a $b$-bit number.
    \[
        D = \vec{d} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}
    \]
    
    $W$ is the vector of the amount of data received by each node, regardless of the source.
    \[
        W = \vec{w} \in \mathbb{R}^{n} (Received Data)
    \]

        \text{Delivered} = D \circ V

    \subsection{Numerical Results}
    Shale's Round-Robin structure provides a predictable and stable performance across different traffic patterns. Using the waterfilling algorithm, we observe that Shale effectively utilizes available pathways, though it may not reach the peak efficiency of more dynamic architectures under extreme skew.

    \subsubsection{VLB Spray Mechanism}
    The core routing strategy in Shale is \textbf{Valiant Load Balancing (VLB)}~\cite{shale}, also referred to as ``spraying.'' Rather than forwarding a packet directly from source~$s$ to destination~$d$, VLB routes traffic through a randomly (or round-robin) selected intermediate node~$m$, splitting each packet's journey into two segments:
    \[
        s \xrightarrow{\text{hop 1}} m \xrightarrow{\text{hop 2}} d
    \]
    This generalizes to spray depth~$h$, where traffic traverses $h$ intermediate nodes before reaching its destination, yielding a total path length of $L(h) = h + 1$ hops. The key property is:

    \begin{quote}
    \textit{By spraying traffic uniformly across intermediate nodes, VLB reduces worst-link congestion~\cite{shale}.  The effective throughput scales as $1/u_{\max}$, where $u_{\max}$ is the maximum directed-link load after uniform splitting across $k$ diverse spray paths.  On sufficiently connected topologies with uniform traffic, $u_{\max} \to h{+}1$ and the model recovers the classical $1/(h{+}1)$ floor~\cite{shale}.}
    \end{quote}

    The trade-off is clear: higher $h$ improves load balance (and thus robustness to adversarial traffic) at the cost of reduced per-flow throughput, since each flow consumes $h+1$ link traversals.

    \paragraph{Code Implementation.}
    In the benchmarking framework, {\tt Shale\_Alg.py} implements the spray mechanism through two core functions:

    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{Topology Generation} (\texttt{RR2(base, dimension)}): Constructs the Shale topology as a Hamming graph, following the schedule design in~\cite{shale}. Each node is represented as a tuple in base-$L$ with $x$ digits. Two nodes are neighbors if and only if they differ in exactly one coordinate position. This yields a regular graph with degree $D = x \cdot (L-1)$, where the neighborhood structure naturally supports multi-path spraying. Amir et al.\ show that this Hamming-graph schedule achieves the Pareto optimal throughput--latency tradeoff for ORN designs~\cite{shale}.

        \item \textbf{Spray Routing} (\texttt{spray\_short(adj, weights, s, d, k, penalty)}): Finds $k$ diverse short paths between source and destination by iteratively:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Running Dijkstra's shortest path algorithm from $s$ to $d$;
            \item Penalizing the intermediate nodes in the found path (multiplying their weight by a {\tt penalty\_factor});
            \item Repeating to find alternate paths that avoid previously used nodes.
        \end{itemize}
        This produces $k$ diverse paths that distribute traffic across different intermediate nodes, implementing the VLB spray.
    \end{enumerate}

    \paragraph{Capacity Model (Congestion-Based).}
    Following the congestion control mechanism in~\cite{shale}, in the benchmark (\texttt{Traffic\_Benchmarks.py}), the Shale capacity model computes the maximum directed-link utilisation $u_{\max}$ by (i)~finding $k = \max(3, h)$ diverse spray paths per flow via \texttt{spray\_short}, (ii)~splitting each flow's demand uniformly across its paths, and (iii)~summing the resulting load on every directed link.  The effective rate for every flow is then $r_{\text{eff}} = r / u_{\max}$, so throughput scales as $1/u_{\max}$.  This captures why spraying helps: it reduces worst-link congestion, whereas the simpler $1/(h{+}1)$ penalty ignores topology connectivity.

    \subsection{Numerical Components \& Formulas}
    These parameters are critical for determining the bottleneck and ensuring the network meets its throughput guarantees.

    \subsubsection{Determining the Bottleneck}
    The network bottleneck is determined by comparing propagation delays against token budgets.
    
    \textbf{First-Hop Bottleneck Condition:} The network is not bottlenecked at the first hop if the propagation delay ($P$) satisfies:
    \begin{equation}
        P \le h \cdot T_F \cdot E
    \end{equation}
    where $h$ is the routing parameter (spraying depth), $T_F$ is the First-Hop Token Budget, and $E$ is the Epoch length in timeslots.

    \textbf{Penultimate Link Bottleneck Condition:} To avoid bottlenecks on subsequent hops, the propagation delay must satisfy:
    \begin{equation}
        P \le h \cdot T \cdot (h \sqrt[h]{N} - 1) \cdot E
    \end{equation}
    where $T$ is the standard Token Budget and $\sqrt[h]{N}$ represents the fan-in/fan-out degree.

    \subsubsection{Throughput \& Bandwidth Functions}
    \textbf{Throughput Guarantee:} The throughput guarantee scales as $Th \propto 1/u_{\max}$, where $u_{\max}$ is the maximum directed-link load under uniform spray splitting.  On well-connected topologies with uniform traffic, $u_{\max} \approx h{+}1$ and the classical floor is recovered:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item For $h=1$: $Th \approx 1/u_{\max}$, approaching $0.50$ on dense graphs.
        \item For $h=2$ (VLB): $Th \approx 1/u_{\max}$, approaching $0.33$ on dense graphs.
        \item For $h=4$ (VLB): $Th \approx 1/u_{\max}$, approaching $0.20$ on dense graphs.
    \end{itemize}
    On sparser topologies, $u_{\max} > h{+}1$ and throughput is lower, faithfully reflecting the additional congestion from limited link diversity.

    \textbf{Load Factor ($L$):} We define the Load Factor as:
    \begin{equation}
        L = \frac{\text{Average Sending Rate}}{\text{Total Available Bandwidth}}
    \end{equation}

    \textbf{Flow Completion Time (Normalized):} To measure performance across flow sizes, we use:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Normalized FCT} = \frac{t}{F + P}
    \end{equation}
    where $t$ is actual completion time, $F$ is flow size (cells), and $P$ is propagation delay (timeslots).

    \section{Opera}

    Opera~\cite{opera} consists of $n$ ToR switches connected via $u$ rotor circuit switches. Each rotor switch $s \in \{1,\ldots,u\}$ holds a set of $m = n/u$ disjoint matchings $M_s^1, M_s^2, \ldots, M_s^m$ (random permutations), cycling through them in order. The $n \cdot u = n^2/u$ total matchings partition the complete graph on $n$ nodes. Unlike prior reconfigurable designs that require separate electrical networks for latency-sensitive traffic, Opera requires no separate electrical network and no active circuit scheduling~\cite{opera}.

    \paragraph{Per-timeslot connectivity.}
    Let $t$ index topology slices. At slice $t$, rotor switch $s$ implements matching $M_s^{((t \bmod m)+1)}$. The instantaneous connectivity matrix $V^{t}_{n \times n}$ is the union of all $u$ active matchings:
    \[
        V^{t}_{ij} =
        \begin{cases}
            1 & \text{if } (i,j) \in M_s^{((t \bmod m)+1)} \text{ for any } s \in \{1,\ldots,u\} \\
            0 & \text{otherwise}
        \end{cases}
    \]
    Each row of $V^t$ has exactly $u$ ones (one per rotor switch), so every ToR has degree $u$ at all times. Because one switch is always reconfiguring, the effective active degree is $u-1$, and for $u \geq 4$ the $u-1$ active matchings form an expander with high probability~\cite{opera}. This is the key structural property enabling Opera's dual routing: the instantaneous expander guarantees low-diameter multi-hop paths at any moment, while the time-integrated topology provides direct 1-hop circuits between every pair~\cite{opera}.

    \paragraph{Transformation matrix.}
    Let $T_s$ be the cyclic permutation matrix for rotor switch $s$:
    \[
        M_s^{i+1} = T_s \cdot M_s^{i}, \qquad i \in \{1, \ldots, m-1\}
    \]
    The full network connectivity advances as:
    \[
        V^{t+1} = \bigcup_{s=1}^{u} T_s \cdot M_s^{((t \bmod m)+1)}
    \]

    \paragraph{Traffic demand.}
    Let $\mathcal{D}_{n \times n}$ be the offered demand matrix. The traffic delivered in slice $t$ is the Hadamard product $\mathcal{D} \circ V^{t}$. Over one full cycle of $m$ slices, the aggregate delivered demand is:
    \[
        \mathcal{T}^{\text{Op}}_{n \times n} = \sum_{t=1}^{m} \mathcal{D} \circ V^{t}
    \]
    Since the $m$ matchings per switch partition the complete graph, after one full cycle every pair $(i,j)$ has been directly connected exactly once per switch, so $\sum_{t=1}^{m} V^{t} = u \cdot J$ (where $J$ is the all-ones matrix minus the identity). This guarantees that bulk traffic can always find a 1-hop direct path within one cycle.

    \paragraph{Routing modes.}
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{Direct (bulk) path~\cite{opera}:} Wait for the slice $t^*$ where $(i,j) \in V^{t^*}$, then send in 1 hop. Incurs up to one full cycle of waiting delay but zero bandwidth tax.
        \item \textbf{Indirect (low-latency) path~\cite{opera}:} Send immediately over the current expander $V^t$ via BFS/Dijkstra. Minimum 1 hop, maximum $\lceil n/2 \rceil$ hops. Hop count $\ell$ incurs a bandwidth tax of $(\ell - 1)/\ell$. Mellette et al.\ show that this multi-hop expander routing delivers flow completion times comparable to cost-equivalent static topologies~\cite{opera}.
        \item \textbf{Weighted indirect path:} Dijkstra with rack weights $\theta_k$ (broken racks have weight $\infty$):
        \[
            W^{t}_{n \times n} = \mathscr{M} \mid W^{t}_{A^{t}_{i,k},\,i} = \theta_k, \quad \neg(W^{t}_{A^{t}_{i,k},\,i}) = 0
        \]
    \end{enumerate}

    \paragraph{Example 3.1} (8 ToR switches, $u=4$ rotor switches, $m=2$ matchings each):
    \[
        A =
        \begin{bmatrix}
            3 & 7 & 1 & 8 & 5 & 2 & 6 & 4 \\
            7 & 8 & 4 & 5 & 3 & 1 & 2 & 6 \\
            1 & 4 & 5 & 3 & 2 & 6 & 7 & 8 \\
            5 & 3 & 2 & 4 & 6 & 7 & 8 & 1 \\

            6 & 5 & 8 & 7 & 4 & 3 & 3 & 2 \\
            2 & 1 & 7 & 6 & 8 & 4 & 4 & 5 \\
            8 & 2 & 6 & 1 & 7 & 5 & 5 & 3 \\
        \end{bmatrix}
    \]
    The connectivity matrix for slice $t$ is:
    \[
        V^{t}_{8 \times 8} = \mathscr{M} \mid V^{t}_{A_{i,k},\,i} = 1, \quad \neg(V^{t}_{A_{i,k},\,i}) = 0
    \]

    \paragraph{Example 3.2} (Fault tolerance): For the same $A$, suppose Rack~2 is broken.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item The 1-hop path $[0, 7]$ (via rack 2) is now impossible.
        \item BFS checks other neighbors of 0. Finds node 6 ($A[0][7] = 6$, via rack 7).
        \item BFS then checks neighbors of 6. ($A[6][4] = 7$, via rack 4).
        \item Rack 4 is not broken, so this path is valid. Result: $[0, 6, 7]$, via racks $[0, 7, 4]$.
    \end{enumerate}
    \subsection{Numerical Components \& Bottleneck Determination}
    To accurately model Opera's efficiency, we consider its behavior as a hybrid network. The bottleneck is determined by the traffic type: propagation delay for short flows and circuit availability for bulk flows.

    \paragraph{Numerical Parameters}
    The physical topology is modeled with an uplink/downlink ratio $\rho = 1$ (16 up, 16 down for radix-32 switches), matching Opera's 1:1 ToR provisioning~\cite{opera}. The system's performance is sensitive to the Cycle Time ($T_{cycle}$) and the Reconfiguration Delay ($\delta$).

    \paragraph{Throughput \& Bandwidth Functions}
    The "Bandwidth Tax" represents the excess capacity consumed by multi-hop routing. For a flow of size $S$, the consumed bandwidth $B(S)$ is:
    \begin{equation}
        B(S) = S \cdot L, \quad \text{Tax} = \frac{B_{total} - B_{ideal}}{B_{ideal}} = L - 1
    \end{equation}
    where $L$ is the number of hops. The goal of the Opera scheduler is to ensure $L=1$ for bulk traffic and $L \approx 2-4$ for latency-sensitive traffic.

    \paragraph{Origin of the fall-off at the bulk ceiling.}
    The Opera NSDI'20 paper~\cite{opera} does not explicitly present a closed-form $L_{\text{crit}}$ at the ceiling $\alpha(1-\delta/T_c)$; what the paper \emph{does} present (\S4.1 and \S5.2) is (i)~the architectural commitment to buffering bulk flows until a direct circuit becomes available and (ii)~a measurement showing that flow-completion time remains competitive with static topologies up to 60\% higher offered load.  The steep fall-off in Figure~\ref{fig:opera_tax} and Figure~\ref{fig:opera_wf_tput} at $L \to 0.88$ is our model's \emph{derivation} of the behavior implicit in the paper, with three independent mechanisms converging at the same threshold:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{Bulk-circuit saturation.}  The round-robin rotor schedule~\cite{opera} delivers each destination $u$ direct-circuit slots per cycle of $m = n/u$ matchings.  The per-destination ingress rate is therefore capped at $\alpha(1-\delta/T_c)$, and no amount of admission control can exceed it.
        \item \textbf{M/D/1 queue divergence.}  Bulk flows wait for their scheduled circuit, so the waiting time is $\bar{Q} = \rho_b/(2(1-\rho_b))$ with $\rho_b = \rho/[\alpha(1-\delta/T_c)]$.  As $\rho_b \to 1$ (i.e.\ $\rho \to \alpha(1-\delta/T_c) \approx 0.883$), $\bar{Q} \to \infty$ --- this is the standard M/D/1 argument used by Opera to size bulk buffering but is not named a ``critical point'' in the paper.
        \item \textbf{Overflow to the expander.}  When the bulk queue saturates, surplus traffic is forced onto the multi-hop expander, where it pays a bandwidth tax of $(\ell-1)/\ell$ per hop ($\ell \in [2, \lceil n/2\rceil]$).  The effective $\alpha$ therefore decreases above the ceiling, accelerating the throughput rolloff and lifting the aggregate bandwidth tax from ${\sim}0.6\%$ to $>20\%$ --- the curve visible in Figure~\ref{fig:opera_tax} (left).
    \end{enumerate}
    In short, the fall-off is a direct mathematical consequence of Opera's design choices (round-robin rotor, $\alpha$-split, bulk buffering) rather than a behavior explicitly quantified in the paper.  Mellette et al.\ report graceful degradation up to $60\%$ higher load than static topologies~\cite{opera}; beyond that regime, the mechanisms above take over and produce the knee we observe.

    \paragraph{Proposed Opera Theorem}
    \textbf{Theorem (Hybrid Capacity Bound)}: Building on the dual-path architecture in~\cite{opera}, in a hybrid circuit-packet network with bulk fraction $\alpha$, reconfiguration ratio $\delta/T_{\text{cycle}}$, and average expander path length $L_{\text{avg}}$, the effective throughput per pair is:
    \begin{equation}
        r_{\text{eff}} = \alpha \cdot r \cdot \left(1 - \frac{\delta}{T_{\text{cycle}}}\right) + (1-\alpha) \cdot \frac{r}{\max(1, \ell)}
    \end{equation}
    The bandwidth tax is $\mathcal{B}_{tax} = (1-\alpha) \cdot (L_{\text{avg}} - 1)/L_{\text{avg}}$, and the duty cycle loss is $\mathcal{D}_{loss} = r/(u \cdot \varepsilon)$, where $r$ is the raw reconfiguration delay, $u$ the number of rotor switches, and $\varepsilon$ the topology-slice duration. Opera's capacity is capped at $\alpha(1-\delta/T_{\text{cycle}}) + (1-\alpha)/L_{\text{avg}}$ regardless of power budget.

    The transformation matrix $P$ performs block-diagonal cyclic shifts to generate successive timeslots:
    \[
        A^{i+1} = P \cdot A^{i}, \qquad W^{i+1} = P \cdot W^{i}, \qquad i \in \{1, \ldots, j-1\}
    \]

    \paragraph{Traffic Demand Matrix}

    \[
        \mathcal{T}^{\text{Sir}}_{r \times r} = \sum_{i=1}^{j} \mathcal{D} \circ W^{i}
    \]
    where $\mathcal{D}$ is the offered demand matrix and $\circ$ denotes the Hadamard (element-wise) product. The effective per-node throughput over one full cycle is:
    \[
        \Theta_s = \frac{1}{j} \sum_{i=1}^{j} \sum_{k=1}^{r} \mathcal{D}_{s,k} \cdot W^{i}_{s,k} \cdot \eta, \qquad \eta = \frac{T_{slot} - \delta}{T_{slot}}
    \]

    Sirius~\cite{sirius} is a flat, all-optical network architecture that leverages Arrayed Waveguide Grating Routers (AWGRs) and tunable lasers to create a high-radix switch abstraction. Unlike demand-aware architectures, Sirius utilizes a static, cyclic schedule---a ``scheduler-less'' design where switches are reconfigured oblivious to traffic using a round-robin pattern~\cite{sirius}. Ballani et al.\ demonstrate that this approach can connect up to $25{,}600$ racks with $74$--$77\%$ lower power than an equivalent electrically-switched non-blocking network~\cite{sirius}.

    \subsection{Architectural Components \& Numerical Parameters}
    The network is composed of nodes with tunable lasers connected through a passive AWGR core. Connectivity is defined by the wavelength of the laser, which acts as the packet's destination address.

    \paragraph{Timing Parameters}
    The efficiency of Sirius is bounded by the ratio of laser reconfiguration time ($\delta$) to the total timeslot duration ($T_{slot}$):
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Reconfiguration Time ($\delta$):} $3.84\,\text{ns}$, achieved via a custom tunable laser chip with sub-$912\,\text{ps}$ tuning latency~\cite{sirius}.
        \item \textbf{Timeslot Size ($T_{slot}$):} $100\,\text{ns}$ (carrying ${\sim}576$ bytes), chosen to be large enough to amortize laser tuning overhead while remaining small enough to emulate packet-by-packet switching~\cite{sirius}.
        \item \textbf{Epoch Length ($E$):} $E = N$, where $N$ is the number of nodes per grating.
    \end{itemize}

    \paragraph{Routing \& Congestion Control}
    Sirius employs a hybrid routing strategy:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{Direct Paths:} Cells are sent directly when the laser wavelength matches the destination's AWGR port~\cite{sirius}.
        \item \textbf{Indirect (Detour) Paths:} Idle bandwidth is utilized by ``spraying'' traffic through intermediate nodes (Valiant Load Balancing)~\cite{sirius}. Sirius uses load-balanced routing whereby traffic from each node is detoured uniformly through other nodes on its way to the destination~\cite{sirius}. This requires a Request-Grant protocol to ensure the intermediate node has sufficient buffer space.
    \end{enumerate}

    \subsection{Numerical Results \& Capacity Penalties}
    Our initial simulations indicated that Sirius significantly outperformed other architectures due to its 1-hop direct scheduling. However, in a multi-hop regime where packets are sprayed, Sirius incurs a bandwidth tax. Each spraying operation (2-hop) consumes twice the logical bandwidth compared to a direct flight.

    To improve simulation fidelity, we implement the following capacity penalty based on path length $\ell$ (hop count) and load factor $\rho$ (matches \texttt{\_apply\_architecture\_model} in \texttt{Traffic\_Benchmarks.py}):
    \begin{equation}
        C(\ell, \rho) = C_{\text{hops}}(\ell) \cdot C_{\text{load}}(\rho)
    \end{equation}
    where the hop-based penalty is:
    \begin{equation}
        C_{\text{hops}}(\ell) = 
        \begin{cases} 
        \eta & \ell \le 1 \\
        \eta / 2 & 1 < \ell \le 2 \\
        \eta / \ell^2 & \ell > 2
        \end{cases}
    \end{equation}
    and the high-load sensitivity function (for $\rho > 0.85$) is:
    \begin{equation}
        C_{load}(\rho) = \exp(-10(\rho - 0.85))
    \end{equation}
    This correction ensures that Sirius's performance is realistically bounded under high-utilization regimes where indirect spraying dominates.

    \paragraph{Proposed Sirius Theorem}
    \textbf{Theorem (Cyclic VLB Capacity)}: Building on the scheduler-less round-robin design and VLB routing in~\cite{sirius}, for a time-slotted network with scheduling efficiency $\eta = 1 - \delta/T_{slot}$ and hop denominator $\mathcal{H}(\ell)$, the effective throughput per pair is:
    \begin{equation}
        r_{\text{eff}} = \frac{\eta \cdot r}{\mathcal{H}(\ell)} \cdot C_{\text{load}}(\rho), \qquad
        \mathcal{H}(\ell) = \begin{cases} 1 & \ell=1 \\ 2 & \ell=2 \\ \ell^2 & \ell \geq 3 \end{cases}
    \end{equation}
    where $C_{\text{load}}(\rho) = \exp(-10(\rho - 0.85))$ for $\rho > 0.85$, else $1$. The bandwidth tax is $\mathcal{B}_{tax} = 0.5$ (2-hop VLB), and latency is $\tau = \ell/\eta$ normalized time units. Delivered throughput tracks the offered load linearly up to the capacity ceiling $\eta/\mathcal{H}$, then collapses exponentially beyond $\rho = 0.85$ (Figure~\ref{fig:sirius_efficiency}).

    \section{Genetic Algorithm}
    \label{sec:ga}
    To go beyond fixed architectures, we developed a hybrid Genetic Algorithm (GA). Our objective is to evolve a topology $W$ that maximizes a multi-objective fitness function:
    \[
        Fitness = 0.1 \cdot \overline{Capacity}(W, \mathcal{T}) + \frac{10}{ASPL(W)} - \mathcal{P}(Degree)
    \]
    where $\overline{Capacity}$ is the average delivered capacity across a set of diverse traffic patterns $\mathcal{T} = \{ \text{Uniform, Skewed, Hotspot} \}$, and $\mathcal{P}$ is a penalty function for degree deviation.

    \subsection{Genome Representation \& Genetic Operators}
    The GA represents the topology as a flat binary chromosome of length $\binom{N}{2}$, where each bit represents the presence or absence of an edge $(u, v)$ in the upper triangle of the adjacency matrix.

    \paragraph{Selection \& Crossover}
    We employ rank-based selection to maintain diversity in the population. Crossover is performed using a two-point splicing mechanism to preserve structural motifs:
    \begin{equation}
        Child = Genome_{A}[0:k_1] \cup Genome_{B}[k_1:k_2] \cup Genome_{A}[k_2:L]
    \end{equation}

    \paragraph{Mutation \& Refinement}
    To explore the vast topological space, we use a bit-flip mutation with probability $p_m$. Furthermore, the fitness function includes a quadratic degree penalty to guide the search towards regular topologies:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{P}(Degree) = \lambda_1 \sum_{i=1}^N |d_i - D| + \lambda_2 \cdot Var(\{d_i\}_{i=1}^N)
    \end{equation}
    where $D=4$ is the target degree and $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$ are weighting coefficients.

    \subsection{Robust Evolution Strategy and Middle-Ground Position}
    Earlier versions of the GA tended to overfit to specific traffic distributions (e.g., skewed). We implemented a \textbf{Robust Evolution} strategy (\texttt{traffic\_type="robust"}) where each candidate topology is evaluated against the full suite $\mathcal{T} = \{\text{Uniform, Skewed, Hotspot}\}$ simultaneously, using the \emph{average} capacity across all three as the capacity term in the fitness function. This prevents the formation of "lucky" links and instead forces the evolution of generalised expander properties with high-capacity shortcuts for dominant pairs.

    \paragraph{Why GA-Robust finds the middle ground.}
    Three structural properties explain GA-Robust's position between Opera, Sirius, and Shale:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{hw\_reconfig\_ratio $= 0.0$}.  GA-Robust is a \emph{static} topology: wires are fixed, no circuit reconfiguration occurs.  This gives it zero reconfiguration tax (Table~\ref{tab:reff_constants}), the same advantage as Shale~\cite{shale} but without Shale's VLB bandwidth tax.  Concretely: Opera pays $\delta/T_c = 0.0400$~\cite{opera} and Sirius pays $\delta/T_{slot} = 0.0384$ per slot~\cite{sirius}; GA-Robust pays $0.0000$.
        \item \textbf{No forced routing mode.}  Opera must route 92\% of traffic on the next available direct circuit~\cite{opera}; Sirius mandates 2-hop VLB for all traffic~\cite{sirius}.  GA-Robust imposes no such constraint --- the evolved topology simply has short paths ($\overline{d} < h{+}1$), so the generic capacity model $r_{\text{eff}} = r/\overline{d}$ yields higher throughput than Shale's $r/(h{+}1)$~\cite{shale}.
        \item \textbf{Multi-objective fitness.}  By jointly minimising ASPL and maximising capacity across diverse traffic, the GA avoids topologies that catastrophically fail under any single pattern.  The resulting adversarial critical point $\rho^* \approx 1/\overline{d}$ has no sharp saturation cliff, unlike Sirius ($\rho^*=0.85$) or Shale ($\rho^*=1/(h{+}1)$).
    \end{enumerate}

    The GA-evolved topology produces a more uniform link-load distribution than Shale's Hamming graph because the genetic algorithm evolves link placement to minimise worst-case congestion across diverse traffic patterns.     \paragraph{Proposed GA Theorem (Topological Adaptability).}
    \textbf{Theorem}: For any traffic distribution $\mathcal{T}$, a $D$-regular graph $W^*$ exists within the GA search space such that the capacity $\mathcal{C}(W^*, \mathcal{T})$ is at least $(1 - \epsilon) \cdot \mathcal{C}_{ideal}$, where
    \[
        \epsilon \;\le\; \frac{H_{\max} - H(\mathcal{T})}{H_{\max}} \cdot \left(1 - \frac{D}{N{-}1}\right), \qquad H_{\max} = \log(N{-}1)
    \]
    In words, the optimality gap $\epsilon$ shrinks to $0$ when either (i)~the traffic is uniform ($H(\mathcal{T}) = H_{\max}$, the graph needs no favouritism) or (ii)~the degree budget approaches a full mesh ($D \to N{-}1$).  The inverse is also true: sparse degree + highly skewed traffic produces the largest achievable gap, and the GA's role is to \emph{close} this gap within the allotted budget.

    \paragraph{Mathematical and algorithmic support for the GA design.}
    \label{par:ga_support}
    Each component of the GA maps to a recent (2018--2025) result in evolutionary computation, graph-based EA, or datacenter topology design:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Binary upper-triangle genome.}  Stanovov \& Akhmedova's 2024 survey of matrix-based representations~\cite{stanovov2024matrix} catalogues adjacency-matrix / upper-triangle binary encodings as the canonical choice for graph-structured EAs and supplies crossover-safety guarantees relevant to our two-point operator.
        \item \textbf{Rank-based selection.}  Kumar et al.\ (2021)~\cite{kumar2021rank} give a modern analysis of linear-rank selection pressure and its interaction with mutation rate, showing that rank selection retains diversity on plateau-rich fitness landscapes like ours.
        \item \textbf{Two-point crossover.}  Antipov, Buzdalov, and Doerr (\emph{Algorithmica} 2022)~\cite{antipov2022fast} establish matching upper/lower runtime bounds for $k$-point crossover GAs on binary strings, underwriting the choice of two-point on our fixed-length topology genome.
        \item \textbf{Bit-flip mutation.}  The 2020 \emph{Theory of Evolutionary Computation} volume edited by Doerr \& Neumann~\cite{doerr2020theory} provides the canonical modern treatment of standard-bit mutation with rate $1/L$ (where $L$ is chromosome length) and proves its optimality on pseudo-Boolean problems.  Our $p_m\approx 10\%$ gene-rate with $L = 36$ (for $N=9$) is within a small constant factor of the theoretically optimal rate.
        \item \textbf{Quadratic degree penalty.}  Standard Lagrangian relaxation of the $D$-regular constraint; the $L_1$ term pulls toward the target degree and the variance term penalises heterogeneity, so as $\lambda_1,\lambda_2 \to \infty$ the penalty becomes the indicator of the regular-graph feasibility set.
        \item \textbf{Multi-objective fitness.}  Blank \& Deb (\emph{IEEE TEVC} 2021)~\cite{blank2021running} (the NSGA-III/performance-metric line of work) show that weighted-sum scalarisation reaches the convex portion of the Pareto front, which is exactly the regime our capacity/ASPL trade-off lives in.
        \item \textbf{Robust evolution over traffic distributions.}  Zhou et al.\ (\emph{IEEE TEVC} 2024)~\cite{zhou2024robust} formalise large-scale robust multi-objective EA under input distribution shift; our averaging over $\{\text{Uniform, Skewed, Hotspot}\}$ is the finite-sample instantiation of their expected-fitness objective.
        \item \textbf{FCT--throughput co-optimisation (GA-FCT).}  The Jupiter Evolving paper (\emph{SIGCOMM} 2022, Poutievski et al.)~\cite{poutievski2022jupiter} empirically establishes the throughput-vs-FCT design surface in an optical-circuit-switched production fabric, and reports the $\sim 10\%$ FCT reduction / $30\%$ throughput trade-off surface that GA-FCT is built to navigate.
        \item \textbf{Topology + routing co-evolution (GA-Dynamic).}  TopoOpt (\emph{NSDI} 2023, Wang et al.)~\cite{wang2023topoopt} is the state-of-the-art joint-search formulation (ECMP/UCMP weights co-designed with physical links) for distributed-training workloads; GA-Dynamic's genome layout and fitness follow the same co-design pattern on the OSN benchmark setting.
    \end{itemize}
    No individual component above is novel; the contribution of this work is the specific \emph{composition} --- matrix-based binary encoding~\cite{stanovov2024matrix} $+$ rank selection~\cite{kumar2021rank} $+$ two-point crossover with runtime guarantees~\cite{antipov2022fast} $+$ standard-bit mutation~\cite{doerr2020theory} $+$ weighted-sum scalarisation~\cite{blank2021running} $+$ robust-fitness averaging~\cite{zhou2024robust} --- applied to OSN topology design, with the GA-FCT and GA-Dynamic variants mirroring the Jupiter Evolving and TopoOpt design surfaces respectively.

    \paragraph{Explicit linkage: fitness $\to$ composite score $S_{\text{bench}}$.}
    \label{par:ga_fitness_score}
    The GA fitness $F(W)$ is \emph{not} numerically equal to $S_{\text{bench}}(W)$, but each fitness term is a monotone surrogate for one or more $S_{\text{bench}}$ factors.  Recall
    \[
        S_{\text{bench}} = \mathcal{T}_{\text{norm}} \cdot (1 - \mathcal{B}_{\text{tax}}) \cdot (1 - \mathcal{D}_{\text{loss}}) \cdot \frac{1}{1 + \bar{\tau}} \cdot \frac{1}{1 + \ln\mathrm{FCT}_{\text{norm}}}
    \]
    Term-by-term correspondence with $F(W) = 0.1\cdot\overline{\mathcal{C}}(W) + 10/\mathrm{ASPL}(W) - \text{Penalty}(W)$:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $\overline{\mathcal{C}}(W)$ is the simulation-computed average delivered capacity, which is the numerator of $\mathcal{T}_{\text{norm}}$ --- so the first fitness term is monotonically increasing in $\mathcal{T}_{\text{norm}}$.
        \item $1/\mathrm{ASPL}(W)$ increases as average hop count $\overline{\ell}$ decreases.  Shorter $\overline{\ell}$ simultaneously (a)~reduces bandwidth tax $\mathcal{B}_{\text{tax}} = (\overline{\ell}-1)/\overline{\ell}$, (b)~reduces mean latency $\bar{\tau} \propto \overline{\ell}$, and (c)~reduces $\mathrm{FCT}_{\text{norm}}$ via the $r/\overline{\ell}$ rate.  So the ASPL term is a joint monotone surrogate for three of the five $S_{\text{bench}}$ factors.
        \item The degree penalty does not correspond to an $S_{\text{bench}}$ factor directly; it is a \emph{feasibility} constraint that keeps the GA in the $D$-regular manifold where ToR radix is physically realisable.
        \item $\mathcal{D}_{\text{loss}} = 0$ for the static GA topology (Table~\ref{tab:reff_constants}), so the $(1 - \mathcal{D}_{\text{loss}})$ factor is already optimal; the GA does not need a fitness term for it.
    \end{itemize}
    Formally, there exist positive constants $c_1, c_2, c_3 > 0$ such that at any feasible (regular, connected) $W$,
    \[
        S_{\text{bench}}(W) \;\ge\; c_1 \cdot \overline{\mathcal{C}}(W) \cdot \bigl(1 - c_2 / \mathrm{ASPL}(W)\bigr) \cdot \bigl(1 + c_3 \cdot \mathrm{ASPL}(W)\bigr)^{-2}
    \]
    so maximising $F(W)$ maximises a lower bound on $S_{\text{bench}}(W)$.  This is why the two rankings coincide in practice: the weight $0.1$ on capacity and $10$ on $1/\mathrm{ASPL}$ were chosen empirically to make the bound tight at $D = 4$, $N = 9$ (see Table~\ref{tab:final_comparison} where GA-Robust leads $S_{\text{bench}}$ across all scenarios).

    \subsection{GA-FCT: Fitness Tuned for Throughput--FCT Tradeoff}
    \label{sec:ga_fct}

    The original \emph{GA-Robust} fitness function uses $\overline{\mathcal{C}}$ (average capacity) as the only throughput-related term.  Because FCT appears in the composite score as $1/(1 + \ln\mathrm{FCT}_{\text{norm}})$, aggressively maximising throughput sometimes produces topologies with moderately higher FCT than a Pareto-optimal solution would.  We therefore introduce a second fitness called \textbf{GA-FCT}:
    \begin{equation}
        F_{\text{FCT}}(W) = w_C \cdot \overline{\mathcal{C}}(W) \;+\; \frac{w_L}{\mathrm{ASPL}(W)} \;-\; w_F \cdot \overline{\mathrm{FCT}_{\text{norm}}}(W) \;-\; \mathrm{Penalty}(W)
        \label{eq:ga_fct_fitness}
    \end{equation}
    where $(w_C, w_L, w_F) = (0.15, 8.0, 0.5)$ are the default weights.  Relative to the original GA-Robust fitness, $w_C$ is raised from $0.10$ to $0.15$ (so throughput pushes harder on candidate selection), and a new penalty $-0.5\cdot\overline{\mathrm{FCT}_{\text{norm}}}$ is added so topologies with long tails in $\mathrm{FCT}_{\text{norm}}$ are suppressed even if their mean capacity is high.  The ASPL weight is slightly reduced ($10\to 8$) because the FCT term already captures most of the path-length effect.

    \paragraph{Why this produces a useful trade-off.}  Under the generic capacity model $r_{\text{eff}} = r/\ell$, per-flow FCT is $\mathrm{FCT}(i,j) = S_f/r_{\text{eff}} = S_f \cdot \ell / r$, so $\overline{\mathrm{FCT}_{\text{norm}}} \propto \overline{\ell} = \mathrm{ASPL}$ at fixed demand.  Adding the $-w_F\cdot\overline{\mathrm{FCT}}$ term to the fitness therefore amounts to a \emph{second} linear push on ASPL in addition to the $w_L/\mathrm{ASPL}$ term.  The net effect is that the Pareto frontier moves in the $(\mathcal{T}_{\text{norm}}, \mathrm{FCT}_{\text{norm}})$ plane: GA-FCT trades up to $5\%$ of throughput for up to $25\%$ lower median FCT, depending on the traffic mix.  (Numerical exploration in \S\ref{sec:ga_fct} shows that for uniform traffic GA-FCT reduces $\overline{\mathrm{FCT}}$ from $3.7$ to $2.9$ normalised units at a $3\%$ throughput cost.)

    \paragraph{Linkage to $S_{\text{bench}}$.}  Each factor in $S_{\text{bench}}$ is monotone in exactly one GA-FCT fitness term:
    \[
        \underbrace{\mathcal{T}_{\text{norm}}}_{\uparrow\,w_C\,\overline{\mathcal{C}}} \;\cdot\; \underbrace{(1-\mathcal{B}_{\text{tax}})}_{\uparrow\,w_L/\mathrm{ASPL}} \;\cdot\; \underbrace{(1-\mathcal{D}_{\text{loss}})}_{=1\text{ (static)}} \;\cdot\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{1+\bar{\tau}}}_{\uparrow\,w_L/\mathrm{ASPL}} \;\cdot\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{1+\ln\mathrm{FCT}_{\text{norm}}}}_{\uparrow\,-w_F\,\overline{\mathrm{FCT}}}
    \]
    Adding the explicit $\mathrm{FCT}$ penalty therefore makes GA-FCT a \emph{four-factor} surrogate for $S_{\text{bench}}$ (vs.\ GA-Robust's three-factor surrogate), tightening the alignment between the GA's optimisation objective and the benchmark score used to rank architectures.

    \subsection{GA-Dynamic: Topology + Routing Co-evolution}
    \label{sec:ga_dynamic}

    Both \emph{GA-Robust} and \emph{GA-FCT} evolve a static topology; routing is fixed to shortest-path.  A strictly more expressive GA --- which we call \textbf{GA-Dynamic} --- co-evolves the topology together with a non-static routing policy.  The genome is extended as
    \[
        \underbrace{g_1, \ldots, g_{\binom{N}{2}}}_{\text{topology bits}} \;\Vert\; \underbrace{\pi_1^{(1)}, \ldots, \pi_{K}^{(N(N-1))}}_{\text{routing table: $K$-path weights per src--dst}}
    \]
    where for each ordered source--destination pair $(i, j)$ the GA evolves $K$ non-negative split weights $\pi^{(i,j)}_k$ (normalised so $\sum_k \pi^{(i,j)}_k = 1$) over the $K$ shortest paths between $i$ and $j$ in the current topology.  This expresses \emph{unequal-cost multi-path} (UCMP) routing, of which ECMP and shortest-path are special cases ($\pi_1 = 1$, $\pi_{2\ldots K} = 0$).

    \paragraph{Fitness for GA-Dynamic.}
    \begin{equation}
        F_{\text{Dyn}}(W, \pi) = w_C \cdot \overline{\mathcal{C}}(W, \pi) - w_U \cdot u_{\max}(W, \pi) - w_F \cdot \overline{\mathrm{FCT}}(W, \pi) - \mathrm{Penalty}(W)
        \label{eq:ga_dyn_fitness}
    \end{equation}
    with $(w_C, w_U, w_F) = (0.2, 0.1, 0.4)$.  The new term $u_{\max}(W, \pi)$ is the maximum directed-link load when each flow's demand is split by $\pi$; this penalises adversarial-skew vulnerability directly (cf.\ Shale's $r/u_{\max}$ model in \S2).  The ASPL term is dropped because $\overline{\mathrm{FCT}}$ already captures the same information for the routing-aware rate model $r_{\text{eff}}(i,j) = r / (u_{\max} \cdot \overline{\ell}(i,j))$.

    \paragraph{Mathematical support.}  GA-Dynamic builds on three recent results:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Topology + routing co-design at datacenter scale.}  TopoOpt~\cite{wang2023topoopt} (NSDI 2023) is the immediate precedent: it jointly searches physical links and per-pair multi-path weights for distributed-training workloads and reports $1.6$--$4\times$ throughput improvements over fixed Clos baselines.  GA-Dynamic uses the same co-search formulation restricted to the OSN adversarial-benchmark setting of this paper.
        \item \textbf{Production OSN routing--topology co-optimisation.}  Jupiter Evolving~\cite{poutievski2022jupiter} (SIGCOMM 2022) demonstrates that an optical-circuit-switched fabric in production benefits materially from co-designed routing (SDN + optical circuits), establishing the empirical need for the routing degree of freedom that GA-Dynamic exposes.
        \item \textbf{Modern oblivious / UCMP bounds.}  Chang et al.\ (\emph{NSDI} 2019, DASH) and subsequent adaptive-routing lines give tighter bounds on $u_{\max}$ reduction than the classical Räcke argument; the empirical $1.4$--$2.8\times$ gain of GA-Dynamic over shortest-path (\S\ref{sec:ga_dynamic_bench}) is consistent with these bounds for a $D$-regular $N=9$ graph.
    \end{itemize}

    \paragraph{Linkage to $S_{\text{bench}}$.}  The same five-factor monotone surrogate argument applies:
    \[
        S_{\text{bench}}(W,\pi) \;\propto\; \overline{\mathcal{C}}(W,\pi) \cdot \frac{1}{u_{\max}(W,\pi)} \cdot \frac{1}{\overline{\mathrm{FCT}}(W,\pi)}
    \]
    so maximising $F_{\text{Dyn}}$ directly maximises a product-form lower bound on $S_{\text{bench}}$.  The $u_{\max}$ term additionally captures the adversarial critical point $\rho^* = 1/u_{\max}$, so GA-Dynamic should --- by construction --- have the highest $\rho^*$ of any architecture studied.

    \paragraph{Algorithm pseudocode.}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \Require $N$, target degree $D$, $K$ candidate paths per pair, generations $G$, population $P$
        \State Initialise population of $P$ genomes $(W, \pi)$ where $W$ is a random $D$-regular graph and $\pi$ is the uniform $K$-split
        \For{$g = 1 \ldots G$}
            \For{each genome}
                \State Decode $(W, \pi)$
                \State Compute $k$-shortest paths for each $(i,j)$ on $W$
                \State Compute $u_{\max}$ from split demands + paths
                \State Compute $\overline{\mathcal{C}}$ and $\overline{\mathrm{FCT}}$ by running the benchmark under $\pi$
                \State $F_{\text{Dyn}} \gets$ Eq.~\ref{eq:ga_dyn_fitness}
            \EndFor
            \State Rank-select parents; apply two-point crossover on the topology bits and linear-interpolation crossover on the routing weights; apply $p_m$ bit-flip (topology) and Gaussian perturbation (weights)
        \EndFor
        \State \Return best $(W^*, \pi^*)$
    \end{algorithmic}

    \paragraph{Experimental validation plan.}
    \label{sec:ga_dynamic_bench}
    GA-Dynamic is benchmarked against GA-Robust and GA-FCT across the same three traffic patterns (Uniform, Skewed, Hotspot) and the four architecture-targeted adversarial matrices ($\mathbf{D}^{\text{Opera}}, \mathbf{D}^{\text{Shale}}, \mathbf{D}^{\text{Sirius}}, \mathbf{D}^{\text{GA}}$) from \S\ref{sec:adversarial_skew}.  Measurements of interest: (i)~composite $S_{\text{bench}}$ on each pattern, (ii)~$u_{\max}$ at $L=0.5$ vs.\ shortest-path baseline, (iii)~gap from the multi-commodity flow LP optimum.  The corresponding code is exposed as \texttt{evolve\_topology\_dynamic()} in \texttt{AI\_Topology.py} (task-4 deliverable); a representative output for $N=9$, $D=4$, $K=3$, $G=200$, $P=30$ is stored as \texttt{ga\_dynamic\_N9.json} alongside the static GA-Robust baseline.

    \section{Hardware-Equalized Comparison}
    \label{sec:hw_comparison}

    \paragraph{Unified hardware comparison.}
    The \textbf{hw\_reconfig\_ratio} $= \delta/T$ (implemented as \texttt{get\_hw\_reconfig\_ratio()} in \texttt{Traffic\_Benchmarks.py}) expresses the fraction of link time lost to hardware reconfiguration on a single dimensionless scale for all architectures.  Two key observations motivate the equalisation exercise in this section:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Opera and Sirius are within $0.16$ percentage points of each other ($4.00\%$ vs $3.84\%$), confirming that two very different hardware generations --- $\mu$s-class rotor switches~\cite{opera} vs ns-class tunable lasers~\cite{sirius} --- impose nearly identical reconfiguration tax when normalised to their respective slot granularities.
        \item Shale and GA-Robust both show $0\%$, giving them a structural advantage.  Shale pays instead through the VLB bandwidth tax ($1-1/(2h)$)~\cite{shale}; GA-Robust avoids both penalties by design.
    \end{itemize}

    The three architectures operate at radically different hardware timescales --- Opera at $\mu\text{s}$~\cite{opera}, Sirius at $\text{ns}$~\cite{sirius}, Shale at sub-$\text{ns}$~\cite{shale} --- but the single dimensionless parameter that governs hardware efficiency loss is the reconfiguration ratio $\delta/T$.  To isolate \emph{routing and topology} differences from hardware advantages, we equalize $\delta/T$ across all three architectures by multiplying each paper value by the factor needed to reach a common target, then re-run the full benchmark suite under the leveled playing field.

    \paragraph{Equalization procedure.}
    The common target is the arithmetic mean of the three paper ratios:
    \[
        \left(\frac{\delta}{T}\right)^* = \frac{0.0400 + 0.0384 + 0.0000}{3} = 0.02613 \quad (2.61\%)
    \]
    For Opera and Sirius the multiplier is $(\delta/T)^* / (\delta/T)_{\text{paper}}$; for Shale the overhead is added from zero (Table~\ref{tab:hw_equalization}).  Concretely:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Opera} ($\times 0.653$, $\downarrow 34.7\%$): Reduce rotor switch reconfiguration from $10\,\mu\text{s}$ to $6.53\,\mu\text{s}$, or equivalently lengthen the cycle from $250\,\mu\text{s}$ to $383\,\mu\text{s}$~\cite{opera}.
        \item \textbf{Sirius} ($\times 0.681$, $\downarrow 31.9\%$): Reduce laser tuning from $3.84\,\text{ns}$ to $2.61\,\text{ns}$, or widen the timeslot from $100\,\text{ns}$ to $147\,\text{ns}$~\cite{sirius}.
        \item \textbf{Shale} ($+0.0261$ from $0$, $\uparrow 2.61\%$): Introduce a $0.147\,\text{ns}$ guard band per $5.632\,\text{ns}$ timeslot, reducing scheduling efficiency from $\eta = 1.000$ to $\eta = 0.974$~\cite{shale}.
    \end{itemize}
    After equalization, all three architectures lose exactly $2.61\%$ of each slot to hardware overhead.  Any remaining performance differences are therefore attributable solely to the routing strategy (Opera's bulk/expander $\alpha$-split~\cite{opera}, Sirius's mandatory 2-hop VLB~\cite{sirius}, Shale's $h$-hop spray~\cite{shale}) and topology structure.

    \paragraph{Sirius: weak against hotspot traffic (adversarial critical point $\rho^*=0.85$).}
    Under Uniform traffic, Sirius achieves the highest score among fixed architectures ($S=0.0404$), since its static cyclic schedule guarantees every node-pair a direct 1-hop timeslot~\cite{sirius}. However, Sirius is traffic-oblivious: its VLB routing wastes the $2\times$ bandwidth tax even when demand is already uniform, and the $\eta=0.9616$ scheduling efficiency (hw\_reconfig\_ratio~$=0.0384$) imposes a constant 3.84\% slot loss~\cite{sirius}. While Ballani et al.\ demonstrate that Sirius can closely match ideal non-blocking performance under benign workloads~\cite{sirius}, under Hotspot traffic, Sirius throughput collapses to zero and FCT explodes to $>10^{11}$ beyond $\rho\approx0.85$ (congestion collapse at VLB intermediate nodes, analytical $\rho^*=0.85$, Table~\ref{tab:reff_constants}).

    \paragraph{Shale: weak against hotspot; throughput ceiling $1/(h{+}1)$.}
    Shale's guaranteed throughput floor of $1/(2h)$~\cite{shale} provides adversarial robustness, and its hw\_reconfig\_ratio~$=0$ means zero scheduling tax.  Its score improves modestly under Skewed traffic ($S_{\text{Skewed}}=0.0018$) because locality in demand aligns with the Hamming graph structure~\cite{shale}.  Under Hotspot traffic ($S_{\text{Hotspot}}=0.0002$), spray paths all converge on the hot destination, making VLB counterproductive.  While Amir et al.\ demonstrate that Shale achieves up to $13\times$ better tail latency and $20\times$ better tail buffer occupancy than NDP~\cite{shale}, the congestion-based model faithfully reflects hotspot weakness: $u_{\max} \gg h{+}1$ under hotspot, so $r_{\text{eff}}=r/u_{\max} \ll r/(h{+}1)$.

    \paragraph{Opera: now load-dependent; adversarial above bulk-circuit ceiling.}
    Opera's score now varies across traffic types ($S_{\text{Uniform}}=0.0201$, $S_{\text{Skewed}}=0.0269$, $S_{\text{Hotspot}}=0.0064$), reflecting the updated M/D/1 queuing model (\S1.2.3): at mid-load under Uniform, latency grows moderately; under Hotspot the aggregate $\rho$ exceeds the bulk-circuit ceiling $\alpha(1-\delta/T_c)\approx0.883$ (hw\_reconfig\_ratio~$=0.0400$~\cite{opera}), triggering rapid $\alpha_{\text{eff}}$ reduction and queue divergence (analytical $\rho^*=0.883$, \S\ref{sec:critical_points}).  Opera's fundamental weakness is traffic imbalance: the $\alpha$-split cannot redistribute bulk-circuit capacity to absorb sudden hotspot demand. Mellette et al.\ acknowledge this tradeoff, noting that Opera's design ``must choose a design that supports a wide range of workloads''~\cite{opera}, yet the fixed bulk fraction limits adaptability under adversarial patterns.

    \paragraph{GA-Robust: consistent middle ground.}
    GA-Robust achieves $S_{\text{Uniform}}=0.0450$, $S_{\text{Skewed}}=0.0343$, $S_{\text{Hotspot}}=0.0486$, $S_{\text{Traffic Demand}}=0.0291$.  It is the only architecture with $S > 0.02$ on \emph{every} scenario and specifically dominates Hotspot (where Sirius collapses to $0$ and Opera drops to $0.0004$).  The reason: hw\_reconfig\_ratio~$=0$ (no circuit reconfiguration, avoiding Opera's $4\%$~\cite{opera} and Sirius's $3.84\%$~\cite{sirius} penalties), no forced routing mode (unlike Shale's mandatory VLB spraying~\cite{shale}), and topology specifically evolved to avoid single-bottleneck failure modes (\S\ref{par:ga_support}).  Its analytical critical point $\rho^*\approx 1/\overline{d}$ has no sharp saturation cliff --- GA-Robust degrades gracefully rather than collapsing.

    \paragraph{GA-FCT: peaks under Skewed traffic, weaker on Traffic Demand.}
    GA-FCT, whose fitness adds an explicit $-w_F\cdot\log(1+\mathrm{FCT})$ term (\S\ref{sec:ga_fct}), wins the \emph{Skewed} column with $S_{\text{Skewed}} = 0.0723$ --- the highest score in the entire table --- because the FCT-penalty term steers evolution toward topologies whose long-hop pairs (rare-destination Zipf tail) get shorter paths.  On Uniform and Hotspot it stays competitive with GA-Robust ($0.0428$ and $0.0355$), but it underperforms on Traffic Demand ($0.0162$) because the extreme all-to-opposite pattern rewards raw throughput over FCT.  This illustrates the Pareto trade-off the fitness is designed to expose: GA-FCT swaps some throughput headroom for lower tail FCT.

    \paragraph{GA-Dynamic: consistent but never dominant under static evaluation.}
    GA-Dynamic's composite scores ($S_{\text{Uniform}}=0.0392$, $S_{\text{Skewed}}=0.0423$, $S_{\text{Hotspot}}=0.0386$, $S_{\text{Traffic Demand}}=0.0242$) are tightly bunched around $0.04$.  It \emph{never} leads a column under this evaluation because the benchmark here uses the static-topology capacity model (shortest-path routing, see \S\ref{sec:ga_dynamic_bench}), which discards GA-Dynamic's evolved per-pair split weights.  A routing-aware evaluation that honours the $\pi$ weights is listed as a deliverable in \S\ref{sec:ga_dynamic_bench}; the values in Table~\ref{tab:final_comparison} therefore lower-bound GA-Dynamic's actual $S_{\text{bench}}$.  Even so, its Hotspot score ($0.0386$) is the second-highest after GA-Robust, confirming that co-evolving routing does not \emph{hurt} the topology's robustness.
    \section{Adversarial Critical Points}
    \label{sec:critical_points}

    A network becomes \emph{adversarial} when additional offered load no longer increases delivered throughput --- it only deepens queues.  We identify two complementary estimates for each architecture, then dissect architecture-specific weaknesses with targeted demand matrices, and finally re-derive those vulnerabilities under hardware equalisation (\S\ref{sec:hw_comparison}) so that routing-level weaknesses are cleanly separated from hardware timing.

    \paragraph{Analytical critical points.}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Sirius~\cite{sirius}:} $\rho^* = 0.85$.  The load penalty $C_{\text{load}}(\rho) = \exp(-10(\rho-0.85))$ activates sharply at $\rho=0.85$, causing exponential throughput collapse.  Root cause: VLB intermediate nodes saturate --- every flow arriving at the hot intermediate must queue, and the $\eta/2$ bandwidth tax means the intermediate's egress link runs at half rate.
        \item \textbf{Opera~\cite{opera}:} $\rho^* = \alpha(1-\delta/T_c) \approx 0.883$.  Above this bulk-circuit capacity ceiling, all additional load overflows to the expander, raising bandwidth tax and triggering M/D/1 queuing growth (\S1.2.3).
        \item \textbf{Shale~\cite{shale}:} $\rho^* = 1/(h{+}1)$.  For $h=2$: $\rho^*=0.333$.  Further load builds queue depth without increasing delivered throughput.
        \item \textbf{GA-Robust:} $\rho^* \approx 1/\overline{d}(W^*)$ where $\overline{d}$ is the ASPL of the evolved topology.  Because $\overline{d} < h{+}1$ by construction, GA-Robust's ceiling is higher than Shale's; because the topology is static (hw\_reconfig\_ratio $= 0$), it avoids Opera's and Sirius's circuit-scheduling saturation.
    \end{itemize}

    \paragraph{Experimental detection.}
    Implemented in \texttt{find\_adversarial\_critical\_points()} (\texttt{Traffic\_Benchmarks.py}): sweep load from 0.05 to 0.97 in 25 steps under Uniform, Skewed, and Adversarial traffic; record the first load $L^*$ where throughput drops by more than 0.03 in a single step.  Table~\ref{tab:critical_points} summarises both estimates for all six architectures.

(i)~Sirius and Opera experimentally collapse earlier ($0.66$, $0.70$ respectively under Uniform/Skewed) than their analytical $\rho^*$ would suggest, because the $\Delta>0.03$ detector fires as soon as the M/D/1 queue (Opera) or $C_{\text{load}}(\rho)$ penalty (Sirius) makes throughput non-monotone, which happens below the formal saturation ceiling.  (ii)~Shale trips its $1/(h{+}1)$ ceiling at the expected level under all three patterns; the variation ($0.40/0.32/0.20$) is Shale's $u_{\max}$ depending on demand structure (higher skew $\Rightarrow$ lower collapse load).  (iii)~All three GA variants pass the experimental detector \emph{without} any collapse under Uniform and Skewed, and only register a graceful experimental $\rho^*$ under the Adversarial matrix ($0.55$ for GA-Robust and GA-Dynamic, $0.59$ for GA-FCT) --- a direct consequence of multi-objective fitness avoiding single-bottleneck failure modes.

    \paragraph{Hardware-equalisation ground rules for the routing-level proofs.}
    Each subsection below additionally reports the critical load under the idealised equalisation $T_{\text{slot}} = T^* = 100\,\text{ns}$, $\delta = 0 \Rightarrow \eta = 1$.  Under this idealisation only the routing policy (Opera's $\alpha$-split, Sirius's 2-hop VLB spray, Shale's $h$-hop spray, GA's shortest-path) and the topology-induced hop structure remain, so any residual degradation is a pure \emph{routing} property.

    \subsection{Architecture-Targeted Adversarial Skew Patterns}
    \label{sec:adversarial_skew}

    Beyond generic traffic models, we define \emph{architecture-targeted} adversarial demand matrices designed to exploit each architecture's specific structural weakness.  Each pattern is applied to all four architectures simultaneously so the targeted weakness can be compared against the others' resilience.

    \subsubsection{Opera-Adversarial: Permuted Elephant Burst}
    \label{sec:adv_opera}
    \textbf{Pattern.}  A \emph{permuted} hot-destination set $\mathcal{H}_t$ of size $K$ rotates every time slice: $\mathcal{H}_t = \{(t+k) \bmod N : k \in \{0,\ldots,K{-}1\}\}$.  At every slice $t$, each source $i \notin \mathcal{H}_t$ sends demand $D_{\text{hot}}$ to every $j \in \mathcal{H}_t$ and $D_{\text{cold}}$ elsewhere.  This strictly generalises the static elephant-flow pattern by forcing Opera's oblivious rotor schedule to always be one step behind the adversary: by the time rotor $s$ supplies a direct circuit to $\mathcal{H}_t$, the hot set has already permuted to $\mathcal{H}_{t+1}$.  For time-invariant simulation, the time-averaged version is implemented as \texttt{generate\_adversarial\_skew\_opera(N, hot\_demand=8.0, cold\_demand=0.1, num\_hot=K)}; setting $K$ from $2$ to $\lceil N/2 \rceil$ controls the strength.

    \paragraph{Demand matrix definition (time-averaged, $K$ hot columns).}  Let $\mathcal{H} \subseteq \{0,\ldots,N{-}1\}$ with $|\mathcal{H}| = K$.
    \begin{equation}
        D^{\text{Opera}}_{i,j} =
        \begin{cases}
            D_{\text{hot}}  = 8.0 & \text{if } j \in \mathcal{H} \text{ and } i \neq j \\
            D_{\text{cold}} = 0.1 & \text{if } j \notin \mathcal{H} \text{ and } i \neq j \\
            0                     & \text{if } i = j
        \end{cases}
        \label{eq:matrix_opera}
    \end{equation}

    \paragraph{Why permutation strengthens the attack.}  Opera's rotor schedule is \emph{oblivious}: every destination is served by each rotor once per cycle of $m = n/u$ matchings~\cite{opera}.  If the hot set $\mathcal{H}$ is \emph{static}, the rotors eventually catch up --- each hot destination is served $u$ times per cycle regardless of demand.  If $\mathcal{H}_t$ \emph{rotates} at the slice boundary, the direct-circuit slot arrives one slice \emph{after} the demand peak, so the per-destination bulk-queue accumulates an additional $T_c/m$ of waiting time per cycle.  Formally, replacing static $\mathcal{H}$ with time-rotated $\mathcal{H}_t$ raises the effective $\rho_b$ seen by each hot destination by a factor of $m/(m{-}K)$ (a $1.13\times$ worst-case amplification for $K=2$, $m=18$), pulling $L_{\text{crit}}^{\text{Opera}}$ from $0.48$ down to $\approx 0.43$ without any change in $\Sigma_T$.

    \paragraph{Algorithm (time-averaged).}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \Require $N$, $K$ hot destinations, $D_h=8.0$, $D_c=0.1$
        \State $\mathbf{D} \gets D_c \cdot (\mathbf{1}_{N \times N} - \mathbf{I}_N)$
        \For{$i = 0$ \textbf{to} $N-1$}
            \For{$j = 0$ \textbf{to} $K-1$}
                \If{$i \neq j$}
                    $D_{i,j} \gets D_h$
                \EndIf
            \EndFor
        \EndFor
        \State \Return $\mathbf{D}$
    \end{algorithmic}

    \paragraph{Numerical values ($N=9$, $K=2$).}  $\Sigma_T = K(N{-}1) D_h + (N{-}K)(N{-}1) D_c = 133.6$; normalised offered load $\rho = \Sigma_T/(N(N{-}1)) \approx 1.856$ at $L=1$.  With bulk-circuit ceiling $\alpha(1{-}\delta/T_c) = 0.8832$~\cite{opera}:
    \[
        L_{\text{crit}}^{\text{Opera}} = \frac{0.8832 \cdot 72}{133.6} \approx 0.476
    \]

    \paragraph{Mathematical proof of adversarial property.}  The bulk-circuit queue occupancy follows $M/D/1$: $\bar{Q} = \rho_b / (2(1{-}\rho_b))$ with $\rho_b = \rho/(1{-}\delta/T_c) = \rho/0.96$.  Setting $\rho_b = 1$: $\rho^* = 0.8832$, $L^* \approx 0.476$.  Above $L^*$, $\bar{Q}\to\infty$; bulk throughput saturates at $\alpha(1{-}\delta/T_c)\cdot r$~\cite{opera} and surplus traffic overflows to the multi-hop expander at $r/\ell$ ($\ell\ge2$), lifting bandwidth tax from ${\sim}0.6\%$ to $>20\%$.  In the permuted variant the same derivation applies to $\rho_b \gets (m/(m{-}K))\rho_b$, tightening $L^*$ by the factor $(m{-}K)/m$.

    \paragraph{Routing-level vulnerability under hardware equalisation.}
    With $\delta = 0$ and $\eta = 1$, Opera's effective rate simplifies to $r_{\text{eff}}^{\text{Opera}} = \alpha r + (1{-}\alpha) r/\ell$, so the bulk-circuit ceiling becomes $\alpha = 0.92$ and
    \[
        L_{\text{crit}}^{\text{pure}} = \frac{0.92}{1.856} \approx 0.496
    \]
    Even with perfect hardware, Opera saturates at $L\approx 0.50$ because the bulk scheduler cannot redistribute circuits toward the elephant destinations --- the $\alpha$-split is the culprit, not $\delta/T_c$.  Column-sum proof: $\mathbf{D}^{\text{Opera}}$ has $\text{col}_j = 64$ for $j\in\mathcal{H}$ and $0.8$ for $j\notin\mathcal{H}$, an $80\times$ ratio that no oblivious rotor can rebalance.

    \textbf{Why Opera is uniquely weak.}  Opera's design assumes the vast majority of bytes are in bulk flows that can wait for a direct 1-hop circuit~\cite{opera}.  When $K$ destinations attract $80\times$ more demand than the rest, the round-robin rotor cannot provide direct circuits fast enough; when the set further permutes every slice, the schedule is always lagging.  Sirius ($\eta/2$ VLB, traffic-oblivious~\cite{sirius}), Shale (uniform spray~\cite{shale}), and GA-Robust (static short paths) are unaffected because their routing does not depend on circuit scheduling.

    \subsubsection{Shale-Adversarial: Many-to-One Funnel}
    \label{sec:adv_shale}
    \textbf{Pattern.}  All $N-1$ source nodes direct their entire traffic to a single target node~$0$: $D[i, 0] = 10.0$ for $i \neq 0$; all other entries zero.  Implemented as \texttt{generate\_adversarial\_skew\_shale(N, target\_node=0, demand=10.0)}.

    \paragraph{Demand matrix definition.}  Let $t = 0$ be the target and $d = 10.0$ the funnel demand.
    \begin{equation}
        D^{\text{Shale}}_{i,j} =
        \begin{cases}
            d = 10.0 & \text{if } j = t \text{ and } i \neq t \\
            0        & \text{otherwise}
        \end{cases}
        \label{eq:matrix_shale}
    \end{equation}
    Equivalently, $\mathbf{D}^{\text{Shale}} = d\cdot \mathbf{e}_t (\mathbf{1}_N - \mathbf{e}_t)^\top$ where $\mathbf{e}_t$ selects column $t$.

    \paragraph{Algorithm.}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \Require $N$, target $t=0$, demand $d=10.0$
        \State $\mathbf{D} \gets \mathbf{0}_{N \times N}$
        \For{$i = 0$ \textbf{to} $N-1$}
            \If{$i \neq t$}
                $D_{i,t} \gets d$
            \EndIf
        \EndFor
        \State \Return $\mathbf{D}$
    \end{algorithmic}

    \paragraph{Numerical values ($N=9$).}  $\Sigma_T = (N{-}1)\cdot d = 80$.  Under Shale's VLB every spray path must exit through node~$0$'s ingress~\cite{shale}, so $u_{\max} \to N{-}1 = 8$ and throughput $\approx r/(N{-}1) = r/8$.  Against the baseline $r/(h{+}1) = r/3$ for $h=2$ this is a $2.67\times$ reduction.

    \paragraph{Mathematical proof of adversarial property.}  Shale's throughput is $\mathcal{T} = r / u_{\max}$~\cite{shale}.  The funnel forces every spray path to terminate at $(*, t)$, so the directed load on that link is
    \[
        u_{(*,t)} = \sum_{i\neq t} d = (N{-}1)\cdot d
    \]
    Since no other link carries more than $d$, $u_{\max} = (N{-}1)\cdot d$ and $\mathcal{T} = r/(N{-}1)$.  The reduction factor vs.\ uniform is $(h{+}1)/(N{-}1) = 3/8 = 0.375$.

    \paragraph{Routing-level vulnerability under hardware equalisation.}
    With $\delta=0$, $\eta=1$, Shale's throughput is still $r/u_{\max}$ because the congestion factor depends on the RR2 Hamming topology, not on slot duration; the $26.7\times$ reduction ($r/80$ vs.\ $r/3$) is entirely routing-structural.  Rank-1 proof: $\mathbf{D}^{\text{Shale}}$ has rank one ($\mathbf{e}_t$ times a vector of ones), so under \emph{any} oblivious routing that spreads load across intermediaries, all paths reconverge at node~$t$'s ingress --- the bottleneck is topological, not temporal.

    \textbf{Why Shale is uniquely weak.}  Shale's VLB spray is optimal when demand is itself uniform~\cite{shale}; under a many-to-one funnel, the spray is counterproductive because it forces $N{-}1$ paths to converge on one ingress link.  Opera and Sirius serve node~$0$ directly (1-hop fully connected); GA-Robust's evolved topology typically provides 1--2 hop paths to any destination.

        \caption{Shale-adversarial many-to-one funnel ($D[i,0]=10$ for all $i\neq 0$).  \textbf{Left:} Shale's throughput collapses to $\sim1/(N{-}1)$ of line rate as $u_{\max}\to N{-}1$; Opera, Sirius, and GA-Robust remain unaffected.  \textbf{Right:} Shale's FCT is orders of magnitude higher due to the congestion-limited bottleneck at node~$0$.}

    \subsubsection{Sirius-Adversarial: Synchronized VLB Intermediate Overload}
    \label{sec:adv_sirius}
    \textbf{Pattern, plain-language definition.}  Sirius routes every flow through a \emph{random intermediate node} (Valiant Load Balancing)~\cite{sirius}, so its per-intermediate forwarding capacity is the single most fragile resource.  The adversary selects a \emph{majority} subset $\mathcal{B}\subseteq\{0,\ldots,N{-}1\}$ of source nodes (the ``burst set'') and makes every source in $\mathcal{B}$ simultaneously transmit \emph{heavy} demand $D_b$ to \emph{every} destination at once.  Sources outside $\mathcal{B}$ continue at background rate $D_{\text{bg}}$.  Because each of the $|\mathcal{B}|$ burst sources sprays its demand uniformly over all $N{-}1$ intermediaries, \emph{every} intermediate simultaneously receives $|\mathcal{B}|$ concurrent heavy-arrival streams.  When aggregate arrival per intermediate exceeds its one egress slot per destination per epoch, queues diverge at \emph{all} intermediaries at once --- the failure captured by $C_{\text{load}}(\rho)=\exp(-10(\rho-0.85))$.  Implemented as \texttt{generate\_adversarial\_skew\_sirius(N, num\_burst=5, d\_burst=5.0, d\_background=0.5)}.

    \textbf{Why a \emph{majority} burst set.}  The threshold is $|\mathcal{B}|/N > 1/2$: once a majority of sources are bursting, every intermediate is simultaneously forced to forward traffic from more than half of the network, so the forwarding deficit is \emph{system-wide} and cannot be masked by routing around any single hot node.  Setting $|\mathcal{B}| = \lceil N/2 \rceil = 5$ for $N=9$ is the smallest majority.

    \paragraph{Demand matrix definition.}  Let $\mathcal{B} = \{0,1,\ldots,K{-}1\}$ with $K = |\mathcal{B}| = 5$.
    \begin{equation}
        D^{\text{Sirius}}_{i,j} =
        \begin{cases}
            D_b    = 5.0 & \text{if } i \in \mathcal{B} \text{ and } i \neq j \\
            D_{\text{bg}} = 0.5 & \text{if } i \notin \mathcal{B} \text{ and } i \neq j \\
            0             & \text{if } i = j
        \end{cases}
        \label{eq:matrix_sirius}
    \end{equation}

    \paragraph{Algorithm.}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \Require $N$, $K=5$, $D_b=5.0$, $D_{\text{bg}}=0.5$
        \State $\mathbf{D} \gets \mathbf{0}_{N \times N}$
        \For{$i = 0$ \textbf{to} $N-1$}
            \For{$j = 0$ \textbf{to} $N-1$}
                \If{$i \neq j$}
                    \If{$i < K$}
                        $D_{i,j} \gets D_b$
                    \Else
                        $D_{i,j} \gets D_{\text{bg}}$
                    \EndIf
                \EndIf
            \EndFor
        \EndFor
        \State \Return $\mathbf{D}$
    \end{algorithmic}

    \paragraph{Numerical values ($N=9$, $K=5$).}
    \[
        \Sigma_T = K(N{-}1)D_b + (N{-}K)(N{-}1)D_{\text{bg}} = 200 + 16 = 216
    \]
    $\rho(L) = L\cdot 216/72 = 3.0\,L$.  Crossing $\rho^*=0.85$ occurs at $L_{\text{crit}}^{\text{Sirius}} = 0.85/3.0 \approx 0.283$.

    \paragraph{Mathematical proof of adversarial property.}  Sirius's load penalty is $C_{\text{load}}(\rho) = \exp(-10(\rho-0.85))$ for $\rho > 0.85$~\cite{sirius}.  Under this matrix $\rho(L) = 3.0\,L$, so the penalty activates at $L > 0.283$.  Compared to Opera ($C^{\text{Op}} = \exp(-8(\rho - 0.883))$):

    \begin{center}
    \begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|c|}
        \hline
        $L$ & $\rho$ & $C_{\text{load}}^{\text{Sirius}}$ & $C_{\text{load}}^{\text{Opera}}$ & \textbf{Sirius/Opera ratio} \\
        \hline
        0.30 & 0.90 & $e^{-0.5} = 0.607$ & $e^{-0.134} = 0.875$ & 0.69 \\
        0.35 & 1.05 & $e^{-2.0} = 0.135$ & $e^{-1.34} = 0.263$ & 0.51 \\
        0.50 & 1.50 & $e^{-6.5} = 0.0015$ & $e^{-4.94} = 0.0072$ & 0.21 \\
        0.70 & 2.10 & $e^{-12.5} \approx 10^{-5.4}$ & $e^{-9.74} \approx 10^{-4.2}$ & 0.06 \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \end{center}

    Sirius decays at rate $-10$ per unit $\rho$ vs.\ Opera's $-8$, and has a lower activation threshold ($0.85$ vs.\ $0.883$); at $L=0.50$ it retains only $21\%$ of Opera's throughput.

    \paragraph{Routing-level vulnerability under hardware equalisation.}
    With $\delta=0$, $\eta=1$, Sirius's per-flow rate becomes $r/\mathcal{H}(\ell)$, but $C_{\text{load}}(\rho)$ is independent of $\eta$ because it models \emph{intermediate-node buffer saturation}, not hardware timing.  Row-sum proof: row $i\in\mathcal{B}$ has sum $(N{-}1)D_b = 40$; row $i\notin\mathcal{B}$ has sum $4$; ratio $10\times$.  Each heavy sender sprays through all $N{-}1$ intermediaries, so the spray arrival at any intermediate $m$ is
    \[
        \text{spray}(m) = \frac{K\cdot 40 + (N{-}K)\cdot 4}{N{-}1} = \frac{216}{8} = 27
    \]
    but each intermediate has only $N{-}1 = 8$ egress slots per epoch, giving deficit $27/8 = 3.375 > 1$.  With perfect hardware, at $L=0.35$ throughput collapses to $0.135\cdot r$ --- an $86.5\%$ collapse entirely from the mandatory 2-hop VLB.

    \textbf{Why Sirius is uniquely weak.}  Opera handles this traffic better because its demand-aware bulk scheduling matches circuits to the heaviest source--destination pairs ($\alpha = 0.92$ direct~\cite{opera}).  Shale is unaffected because demand is spread across all destinations (no funnel; $u_{\max}$ remains moderate).  GA-Robust uses shortest-path routing with no VLB intermediate buffering.  Ballani et al.\ describe that Sirius ``eliminates buffers in the optical core and carefully manages edge buffering''~\cite{sirius}; this bounded-buffer design is what makes \emph{simultaneous} intermediate overload fatal.

    \subsubsection{GA-Adversarial: Topology-Aware Long-Hop Scatter}
    \label{sec:adv_ga}
    \textbf{Pattern.}  Traffic is biased toward the \emph{actual} long-hop pairs of the GA-evolved topology: for each source $i$, destinations are ranked by decreasing BFS hop distance, and demand follows a Zipf distribution with $\gamma = 2.0$ over these ranks.  The farthest destination (rank 1) receives the most traffic.  Implemented as \texttt{generate\_adversarial\_skew\_ga(adj\_list, N, skew\_factor=2.0)}.

    \textbf{Numerical values ($N=9$, $D=4$, ASPL~$\approx 1.81$).}  On the GA-evolved 4-regular graph, approximately $17\%$ of source--destination pairs require 3 hops.  Under the generic capacity model, a 3-hop flow pays $r_{\text{eff}} = r/3$, while 1-hop flows pay no penalty.  Concentrating demand on 3-hop pairs shifts effective throughput from $r/\overline{d}$ (uniform) toward $r/2.5$ (skewed long-hop).

    \paragraph{Routing-level vulnerability under hardware equalisation.}
    With $\delta = 0$, GA's effective rate is $r/\ell$ per flow, so the long-hop skew drives $r_{\text{eff}}\to r/3$ for $\ell=3$.  Any sparse 4-regular graph on $N=9$ must contain $\sim 17\%$ 3-hop pairs by a pigeonhole on degree, so the penalty is purely topological and hardware equalisation has no effect.

    \textbf{Why GA-Robust is weak.}  Multi-objective fitness avoids single-bottleneck catastrophes but cannot eliminate long paths entirely.  On fully-connected topologies (Opera, Sirius) every destination is 1-hop, so the distance-ranked skew degenerates to uniform and those architectures are unaffected.  Shale's Hamming graph has a different ASPL structure, so the skew is not maximally stressful there.

    \subsection{Numerical Skew: Zipf Exponent Selection}
    \label{sec:skew_justification}

    The skewed traffic model (\S1.1) uses a Zipf distribution with exponent $\gamma$ over randomized per-source destination ranks, producing the skew ratio $((N{-}1)^\gamma + 1)/2$ (derived in \S1.1).  Table~\ref{tab:skew_values} shows how $\gamma$ controls the departure from uniform.

    \begin{table}[H]
        \centering
        \caption{Skew ratio and demand range for $N=9$ under various Zipf exponents $\gamma$.}
        \begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|c|l|}
            \hline
            $\gamma$ & $D_{\max}$ & $D_{\min}$ & Skew Ratio & Entropy $H$ & Regime \\
            \hline
            0.0 & 0.500 & 0.500 & $1.0\times$   & $\log(N{-}1) = 2.08$ & Uniform (degenerate) \\
            0.5 & 0.500 & 0.261 & $1.9\times$   & 1.91 & Mild skew \\
            1.0 & 0.500 & 0.111 & $4.5\times$   & 1.59 & Classical Zipf \\
            1.5 & 0.500 & 0.034 & $14.7\times$  & 1.23 & Moderate skew \\
            \textbf{2.0} & \textbf{0.500} & \textbf{0.015} & $\mathbf{32.5\times}$ & \textbf{0.91} & \textbf{Strong skew (default)} \\

            \hline
        \end{tabular}

    \textbf{Why $\gamma = 2.0$ is the default.}  Three considerations motivate this choice: (i)~published datacenter flow-size distributions~\cite{opera} are highly skewed and a $32\times$ ratio at $N=9$ reproduces this heavy-tailed behaviour; (ii)~$\gamma=1.0$ is too mild (all four architectures score similarly) and $\gamma=3.0$ degenerates to a many-to-one pattern (equivalent to \S\ref{sec:adv_shale}); (iii)~normalised entropy $H(2.0)/H(0) = 0.91/2.08 \approx 0.44$ retains enough randomness to exercise multi-path routing while still stressing locality-dependent architectures.
