    \section{Benchmarking Methodology}
    We evaluate our network architectures using a unified benchmarking framework that implements a complete feedback loop: \textbf{Benchmark Scenario $\to$ Experiment Configuration $\to$ Metrics $\to$ Benchmark Score}.

    \subsection{Benchmark Scenario}
    The network is stressed under various traffic models and failure conditions:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Traffic Models} (implemented in \texttt{Traffic\_Benchmarks.py}): Demand matrix $D[i,j]$ for $i \neq j$; $D[i,i]=0$.
            \begin{itemize}
                \item \textbf{Uniform}: $D[i,j] = 1$ (all pairs equal).
                \item \textbf{Skewed}: $D[i,j] = 1 / (|i - j|^\gamma + 1)$, $\gamma$ skew exponent (default 2.0).
                \item \textbf{Hotspot}: $D[i,j] = \omega$ if $i \in \mathcal{H}$ or $j \in \mathcal{H}$, else $1$; $\mathcal{H}$ hotspot nodes, $\omega$ intensity (e.g.\ 10).
                \item \textbf{Adversarial}: $D[i, j(i)] = N$ for $j(i) = (i + \lfloor N/2 \rfloor) \bmod N$, $i \neq j(i)$; all other entries $0$.
            \end{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Load Factor Sweep}: We sweep the load $L$ from light load (0.05) to saturation (0.95).
        \item \textbf{Failure Models}: 
            \begin{itemize}
                \item BROKEN\_NODES: Random node removal (Shale).
                \item BROKEN\_RACKS: Clustered failure of node groups (Opera).
                \item WAVELENGTH\_UNAVAIL: Sparse edge removal in the AWGR core (Sirius).
            \end{itemize}
    \end{itemize}

    \subsection{Performance Metrics Definition}
    We rigorously define the performance metrics used to evaluate each architecture.

    \subsubsection{Normalized Throughput ($\mathcal{T}_{norm}$)}
    In the benchmarking framework (\texttt{Traffic\_Benchmarks.py}), all rates are expressed as a fraction of the per-link line rate $R$, and the implementation takes $R = 1$ without loss of generality. Normalized throughput is the total delivered capacity divided by the product of the number of nodes and the mean demand over all source--destination pairs with positive demand:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{T}_{norm} = \frac{\sum_{(i,j)} r_{\text{eff}}(i,j)}{N \cdot \bar{D}}
    \end{equation}
    where $N$ is the number of nodes, $\bar{D} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}|}\sum_{(i,j) \in \mathcal{F}} D_{i,j}$ is the mean demand (averaged over the set $\mathcal{F}$ of pairs with $D_{i,j} > 0$; $D$ is the demand matrix from \S1.1), and $r_{\text{eff}}(i,j)$ is the effective rate delivered for pair $(i,j)$ after the architecture-specific capacity model.

    \paragraph{Effective rate $r_{\text{eff}}$.}
    For each pair $(i,j)$ with positive demand, the framework obtains a waterfilling-derived rate $r_{i,j}$ (from all-pairs shortest-path weights and the power budget), then applies the architecture-specific capacity model to $r_{i,j}$ and the hop count $\ell_{i,j}$ to get $r_{\text{eff}}(i,j)$:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Shale:} $r_{\text{eff}} = r / u_{\max}$, where $u_{\max} = \max_{(a,b)\in E}\; \sum_{f \ni (a,b)} D_f / k_f$ is the maximum directed-link load when each flow~$f$ with demand~$D_f$ is split uniformly across~$k_f$ diverse spray paths found by penalised Dijkstra (\texttt{spray\_short}).
        \item \textbf{Opera:} $r_{\text{eff}} = \alpha \cdot r \cdot (1 - \delta/T_{\text{cycle}}) + (1-\alpha) \cdot r / \max(1,\ell)$ (bulk fraction $\alpha$, reconfig delay $\delta$, cycle $T_{\text{cycle}}$; short flows penalized by hop count $\ell$).
        \item \textbf{Sirius:} $r_{\text{eff}} = \eta \cdot r$ for 1-hop; $r_{\text{eff}} = (\eta \cdot r)/2$ for 2-hop; $r_{\text{eff}} = (\eta \cdot r)/\ell^2$ for $\ell \geq 3$ (scheduling efficiency $\eta$). If aggregate load $> 0.85$, total capacity is further scaled by $\exp(-10(\text{load}-0.85))$.
    \end{itemize}

    \subsubsection{Flow Completion Time ($FCT_{norm}$)}
    The normalized Flow Completion Time (FCT) measures the time to complete a unit flow relative to the ideal transmission time.
    \begin{equation}
        FCT_{norm} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{F}|} \sum_{f \in \mathcal{F}} \frac{t_{end}(f) - t_{start}(f)}{S_f / R}
    \end{equation}
    where $\mathcal{F}$ is the set of flows, $t$ represents timestamps, and $S_f$ is the flow size.

    \subsubsection{Mean Latency ($\bar{\tau}$)}
    Mean latency captures the average packet delay, including serialization, propagation, and queuing/reconfiguration delays.
    \begin{equation}
        \bar{\tau} = \frac{1}{N_{pkts}} \sum_{p} (T_{prop} + T_{queue} + T_{reconfig})
    \end{equation}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Shale:} $\tau \approx \alpha \cdot \bar{\ell}_{\text{spray}} + \beta$, where $\bar{\ell}_{\text{spray}}$ is the mean hop count across the spray paths actually used.
        \item \textbf{Opera:} $\tau \approx \ell \cdot \delta$ (hop count $\ell$, reconfig delay $\delta$; matches \texttt{Traffic\_Benchmarks.py}).
        \item \textbf{Sirius:} $\tau \approx \ell \cdot T_{slot}$ (hop count $\ell$, slot duration $T_{slot}$).
    \end{itemize}

    \subsubsection{Bandwidth Tax ($\mathcal{B}_{tax}$)}
    Bandwidth tax quantifies the capacity lost to multi-hop routing constraints.
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{B}_{tax} = \frac{\text{Total Capacity Used} - \text{Effective Capacity}}{\text{Total Capacity Used}} = 1 - \frac{1}{\bar{\ell}}
    \end{equation}
    where $\bar{\ell}$ is the average hop count (path length).
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Shale:} $\mathcal{B}_{tax} = 1 - 1/\bar{\ell}_{\text{spray}}$, derived from the mean spray-path hop count $\bar{\ell}_{\text{spray}}$.
        \item \textbf{Opera/Sirius (Indirect):} $\mathcal{B}_{tax} = 1/2$ for 2-hop spraying.
    \end{itemize}

    \subsubsection{Duty Cycle Loss ($\mathcal{D}_{loss}$)}
    Duty cycle loss represents the fraction of time the network is unavailable due to reconfiguration.
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{D}_{loss} = \frac{\delta}{T_{period}}
    \end{equation}
    where $\delta$ is the reconfiguration/locking time and $T_{period}$ is the cycle or slot duration ($T_{\text{cycle}}$ for Opera, $T_{slot}$ for Sirius; see \texttt{ArchitectureParams} in \texttt{Traffic\_Benchmarks.py}). For Shale, $\mathcal{D}_{loss} \approx 0$.

    \subsubsection{Benchmark Score ($S_{bench}$)}
    The composite benchmark score aggregates performance across dimensions to allow single-value comparison.
    \begin{equation}
        S_{bench} = w_1 \mathcal{T}_{norm} + w_2 \frac{\mathcal{T}_{norm}}{1 + \ln(1+\bar{\tau})} + w_3 (1 - \mathcal{U}_{bottleneck}) + w_4 (1 - \mathcal{F}_{rate})
    \end{equation}
    where weights $w_1=0.4$ (Throughput), $w_2=0.3$ (Latency-Efficiency), $w_3=0.2$ (Robustness/Utilization), and $w_4=0.1$ (Degradation under failure).

    \subsection{Numerical Results}
    Shale's Round-Robin structure provides a predictable and stable performance across different traffic patterns. Using the waterfilling algorithm, we observe that Shale effectively utilizes available pathways, though it may not reach the peak efficiency of more dynamic architectures under extreme skew.

    \subsubsection{VLB Spray Mechanism}
    The core routing strategy in Shale is \textbf{Valiant Load Balancing (VLB)}, also referred to as ``spraying.'' Rather than forwarding a packet directly from source~$s$ to destination~$d$, VLB routes traffic through a randomly (or round-robin) selected intermediate node~$m$, splitting each packet's journey into two segments:
    \[
        s \xrightarrow{\text{hop 1}} m \xrightarrow{\text{hop 2}} d
    \]
    This generalizes to spray depth~$h$, where traffic traverses $h$ intermediate nodes before reaching its destination, yielding a total path length of $L(h) = h + 1$ hops. The key property is:

    \begin{quote}
    \textit{By spraying traffic uniformly across intermediate nodes, VLB reduces worst-link congestion.  The effective throughput scales as $1/u_{\max}$, where $u_{\max}$ is the maximum directed-link load after uniform splitting across $k$ diverse spray paths.  On sufficiently connected topologies with uniform traffic, $u_{\max} \to h{+}1$ and the model recovers the classical $1/(h{+}1)$ floor.}
    \end{quote}

    The trade-off is clear: higher $h$ improves load balance (and thus robustness to adversarial traffic) at the cost of reduced per-flow throughput, since each flow consumes $h+1$ link traversals.

    \paragraph{Code Implementation.}
    In the benchmarking framework, {\tt Shale\_Alg.py} implements the spray mechanism through two core functions:

    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{Topology Generation} (\texttt{RR2(base, dimension)}): Constructs the Shale topology as a Hamming graph. Each node is represented as a tuple in base-$L$ with $x$ digits. Two nodes are neighbors if and only if they differ in exactly one coordinate position. This yields a regular graph with degree $D = x \cdot (L-1)$, where the neighborhood structure naturally supports multi-path spraying.

        \item \textbf{Spray Routing} (\texttt{spray\_short(adj, weights, s, d, k, penalty)}): Finds $k$ diverse short paths between source and destination by iteratively:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item Running Dijkstra's shortest path algorithm from $s$ to $d$;
            \item Penalizing the intermediate nodes in the found path (multiplying their weight by a {\tt penalty\_factor});
            \item Repeating to find alternate paths that avoid previously used nodes.
        \end{itemize}
        This produces $k$ diverse paths that distribute traffic across different intermediate nodes, implementing the VLB spray.
    \end{enumerate}

    \paragraph{Capacity Model (Congestion-Based).}
    In the benchmark (\texttt{Traffic\_Benchmarks.py}), the Shale capacity model computes the maximum directed-link utilisation $u_{\max}$ by (i)~finding $k = \max(3, h)$ diverse spray paths per flow via \texttt{spray\_short}, (ii)~splitting each flow's demand uniformly across its paths, and (iii)~summing the resulting load on every directed link.  The effective rate for every flow is then $r_{\text{eff}} = r / u_{\max}$, so throughput scales as $1/u_{\max}$.  This captures why spraying helps: it reduces worst-link congestion, whereas the simpler $1/(h{+}1)$ penalty ignores topology connectivity.

    \subsection{Numerical Components \& Formulas}
    These parameters are critical for determining the bottleneck and ensuring the network meets its throughput guarantees.

    \subsubsection{Determining the Bottleneck}
    The network bottleneck is determined by comparing propagation delays against token budgets.
    
    \textbf{First-Hop Bottleneck Condition:} The network is not bottlenecked at the first hop if the propagation delay ($P$) satisfies:
    \begin{equation}
        P \le h \cdot T_F \cdot E
    \end{equation}
    where $h$ is the routing parameter (spraying depth), $T_F$ is the First-Hop Token Budget, and $E$ is the Epoch length in timeslots.

    \textbf{Penultimate Link Bottleneck Condition:} To avoid bottlenecks on subsequent hops, the propagation delay must satisfy:
    \begin{equation}
        P \le h \cdot T \cdot (h \sqrt[h]{N} - 1) \cdot E
    \end{equation}
    where $T$ is the standard Token Budget and $\sqrt[h]{N}$ represents the fan-in/fan-out degree.

    \subsubsection{Throughput \& Bandwidth Functions}
    \textbf{Throughput Guarantee:} The throughput guarantee scales as $Th \propto 1/u_{\max}$, where $u_{\max}$ is the maximum directed-link load under uniform spray splitting.  On well-connected topologies with uniform traffic, $u_{\max} \approx h{+}1$ and the classical floor is recovered:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item For $h=1$: $Th \approx 1/u_{\max}$, approaching $0.50$ on dense graphs.
        \item For $h=2$ (VLB): $Th \approx 1/u_{\max}$, approaching $0.33$ on dense graphs.
        \item For $h=4$ (VLB): $Th \approx 1/u_{\max}$, approaching $0.20$ on dense graphs.
    \end{itemize}
    On sparser topologies, $u_{\max} > h{+}1$ and throughput is lower, faithfully reflecting the additional congestion from limited link diversity.

    \textbf{Load Factor ($L$):} We define the Load Factor as:
    \begin{equation}
        L = \frac{\text{Average Sending Rate}}{\text{Total Available Bandwidth}}
    \end{equation}

    \textbf{Flow Completion Time (Normalized):} To measure performance across flow sizes, we use:
    \begin{equation}
        \text{Normalized FCT} = \frac{t}{F + P}
    \end{equation}
    where $t$ is actual completion time, $F$ is flow size (cells), and $P$ is propagation delay (timeslots).

    \subsection{Numerical Components \& Bottleneck Determination}
    To accurately model Opera's efficiency, we consider its behavior as a hybrid network. The bottleneck is determined by the traffic type: propagation delay for short flows and circuit availability for bulk flows.

    \paragraph{Numerical Parameters}
    The physical topology is modeled with an uplink/downlink ratio $\rho = 1$ (16 up, 16 down for radix-32 switches). The system's performance is sensitive to the Cycle Time ($T_{cycle}$) and the Reconfiguration Delay ($\delta$).

    \paragraph{Throughput \& Bandwidth Functions}
    The "Bandwidth Tax" represents the excess capacity consumed by multi-hop routing. For a flow of size $S$, the consumed bandwidth $B(S)$ is:
    \begin{equation}
        B(S) = S \cdot L, \quad \text{Tax} = \frac{B_{total} - B_{ideal}}{B_{ideal}} = L - 1
    \end{equation}
    where $L$ is the number of hops. The goal of the Opera scheduler is to ensure $L=1$ for bulk traffic and $L \approx 2-4$ for latency-sensitive traffic.

    Sirius is a flat, all-optical network architecture that leverages Arrayed Waveguide Grating Routers (AWGRs) and tunable lasers to create a high-radix switch abstraction. Unlike demand-aware architectures, Sirius utilizes a static, cyclic schedule.

    \subsection{Architectural Components \& Numerical Parameters}
    The network is composed of nodes with tunable lasers connected through a passive AWGR core. Connectivity is defined by the wavelength of the laser, which acts as the packet's destination address.

    \paragraph{Timing Parameters}
    The efficiency of Sirius is bounded by the ratio of laser reconfiguration time ($\delta$) to the total timeslot duration ($T_{slot}$):
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Reconfiguration Time ($\delta$):} 3.84 ns (state-of-the-art tunable lasers).
        \item \textbf{Timeslot Size ($T_{slot}$):} 100 ns (carrying ~576 bytes).
        \item \textbf{Epoch Length ($E$):} $E = N$, where $N$ is the number of nodes per grating.
    \end{itemize}

    \paragraph{Routing \& Congestion Control}
    Sirius employs a hybrid routing strategy:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{Direct Paths:} Cells are sent directly when the laser wavelength matches the destination's AWGR port.
        \item \textbf{Indirect (Detour) Paths:} Idle bandwidth is utilized by "spraying" traffic through intermediate nodes (Valiant Load Balancing). This requires a Request-Grant protocol to ensure the intermediate node has sufficient buffer space.
    \end{enumerate}

    \subsection{Numerical Results \& Capacity Penalties}
    Our initial simulations indicated that Sirius significantly outperformed other architectures due to its 1-hop direct scheduling. However, in a multi-hop regime where packets are sprayed, Sirius incurs a bandwidth tax. Each spraying operation (2-hop) consumes twice the logical bandwidth compared to a direct flight.

    To improve simulation fidelity, we implement the following capacity penalty based on path length $\ell$ (hop count) and load factor $\rho$ (matches \texttt{\_apply\_architecture\_model} in \texttt{Traffic\_Benchmarks.py}):
    \begin{equation}
        C(\ell, \rho) = C_{\text{hops}}(\ell) \cdot C_{\text{load}}(\rho)
    \end{equation}
    where the hop-based penalty is:
    \begin{equation}
        C_{\text{hops}}(\ell) = 
        \begin{cases} 
        \eta & \ell \le 1 \\
        \eta / 2 & 1 < \ell \le 2 \\
        \eta / \ell^2 & \ell > 2
        \end{cases}
    \end{equation}
    and the high-load sensitivity function (for $\rho > 0.85$) is:
    \begin{equation}
        C_{load}(\rho) = \exp(-10(\rho - 0.85))
    \end{equation}
    This correction ensures that Sirius's performance is realistically bounded under high-utilization regimes where indirect spraying dominates.

    \begin{equation}
        \Theta \approx \sum_{t=1}^{E} \left( \mathbb{I}(\text{Direct}_{u,v,t}) + \frac{1}{2} \cdot \min \left( \mathcal{C}, \sum_{i \in V} \mathbb{I}(\text{Indirect}_{u,i,v,t}) \right) \right)

    \section{Genetic Algorithm}
    To go beyond fixed architectures, we developed a hybrid Genetic Algorithm (GA). Our objective is to evolve a topology $W$ that maximizes a multi-objective fitness function:
    \[
        Fitness = 0.1 \cdot \overline{Capacity}(W, \mathcal{T}) + \frac{10}{ASPL(W)} - \mathcal{P}(Degree)
    \]
    where $\overline{Capacity}$ is the average delivered capacity across a set of diverse traffic patterns $\mathcal{T} = \{ \text{Uniform, Skewed, Hotspot} \}$, and $\mathcal{P}$ is a penalty function for degree deviation.

    \subsection{Genome Representation \& Genetic Operators}
    The GA represents the topology as a flat binary chromosome of length $\binom{N}{2}$, where each bit represents the presence or absence of an edge $(u, v)$ in the upper triangle of the adjacency matrix.

    \paragraph{Selection \& Crossover}
    We employ rank-based selection to maintain diversity in the population. Crossover is performed using a two-point splicing mechanism to preserve structural motifs:
    \begin{equation}
        Child = Genome_{A}[0:k_1] \cup Genome_{B}[k_1:k_2] \cup Genome_{A}[k_2:L]
    \end{equation}

    \paragraph{Mutation \& Refinement}
    To explore the vast topological space, we use a bit-flip mutation with probability $p_m$. Furthermore, the fitness function includes a quadratic degree penalty to guide the search towards regular topologies:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathcal{P}(Degree) = \lambda_1 \sum_{i=1}^N |d_i - D| + \lambda_2 \cdot Var(\{d_i\}_{i=1}^N)
    \end{equation}
    where $D=4$ is the target degree and $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$ are weighting coefficients.

    \subsection{Robust Evolution Strategy}
    Earlier versions of the GA tended to overfit to specific traffic distributions (e.g., skewed). We implemented a "Robust Evolution" strategy where candidates are evaluated against the entire suite of traffic models. This prevents the formation of "lucky" links and instead forces the evolution of generalized expander properties with high-capacity shortcuts for dominant pairs.
